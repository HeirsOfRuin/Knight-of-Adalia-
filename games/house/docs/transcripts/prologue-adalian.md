## Prologue: The Old Lord

> A house is three generations: the man who makes it, the one who keeps it, and the one who spends it.

### The Hall

*Spring, year 11 of King Edwin*

The hall is cold in the mornings now, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the high table yet, and the steward stands at your elbow with the tallies under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

The West is Adalia's. The lords voted it at Lannec eight years ago, and King Edwin rules it from Wendmere through the King's governor at Lannec. Governors come and go. The taxes stay. You were a younger son of nobody in particular once. Now you hold Kerval, and men stand when you come in.

The family comes down to break their fast. Agnès takes the place on your right, as she has for years, and moves the salt so you can reach it. John sits below you, twenty, and eats like someone with somewhere to be. Robert sits beside him and steals from his trencher, the way he has since he could reach.

"The reeve's here about the lower field," says the steward. "And there's a man from Penhoët at the gate. He won't say what he wants. He says he'll wait."

"Let him wait," you say. "He's a Penhoët. They're good at it."

- Ride the bounds with John before the reeve gets you.
- Go over the books with the steward while the fire catches.
- Hear the petitions in the hall, the reeve first.

**You choose:** Ride the bounds with John before the reeve gets you.

You ride out by the mill with John at your stirrup, and show him the stones: the one your men set, the one somebody moved, the one nobody can remember. "That one," you say, "is worth a lawsuit. Remember where it is." He looks at it as if it might get up and walk.

### The Old Company

*Spring, year 11 of King Edwin*

Piers atte Brook comes up from the yard after Terce, the way he has every day since you had a yard. He has gone grey at the temples and bald on top, and he walks with one shoulder lower than the other, from a thing at a bridge you were both too young to be at.

"Your Penhoët's gone," he says. "Left a letter. I told him you'd read it when you'd eaten. He said he'd heard you were a man of appetite."

"And what did you say?"

"I said you'd heard he was a man of letters." He sits without asking, which nobody else in the hall would do, and pours himself your ale. "There's fewer of us at table every Christmas. You've noticed."

You have. You can count the old company on your hands now and have fingers over: the ones who rode with you when you had nothing, who held the line when holding it was stupid, who stood behind you at Lannec. The rest are names you say when the wine is good.

"I'm not asking for anything," says Piers. "I want that said first."

"Then you're the only man in the West who isn't."

"I didn't say I didn't want anything." He turns the cup. "I want to know what happens to us. After."

- Give him a holding of his own, with a hearth he can die at.
- Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.
- Keep him at your side to the end. You will need someone who remembers.

**You choose:** Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.

"John?" He laughs. "He'll not want an old man telling him how you did it." "No," you say. "That's why." He thinks about it, and nods once, and finishes the ale like a man signing something.

### The Heir

*Summer, year 11 of King Edwin*

Haymaking, and the whole manor out in the long meadow with the sun on its neck. You find John at the edge of it, watching the work with his arms folded, counting the cocks of hay and frowning at the ones that are too small.

He is twenty, and taller than you were at that age, and already the haywards watch him the way they watch you. They are trying to see whether there is anything of you in him. So are you.

"Father." He straightens. "You came out."

"I own the field." You lean on the hurdle. "Your mother says I come out to be seen, not to see."

"Do you?"

"Both. One day it'll be you they're looking at." You watch him hear it, and not like it, and hide that he does not like it. "So. What do you want to do with yourself, before then?"

- Give John a manor to run, steward and all, and let him fail at it.
- Send John to the court at Wendmere, to be seen and to learn.
- Keep John at your side, and teach him what you know.

**You choose:** Give John a manor to run, steward and all, and let him fail at it.

"Kerval's outlying farms," you say. "The steward answers to you. When you get it wrong, he'll tell me, and I'll tell you I told you so." He looks at you as if you have handed him a live coal, and then, slowly, as if he might want to hold it.

### Penhoët's Suit

*Summer, year 11 of King Edwin*

The letter the Penhoët man left is a writ, and it is sealed with the Penhoët boar. Hervé de Penhoët sues you before the King's justices for Kerval, which, he says, was held by his grandfather and taken from his house without right.

He comes himself a week later, which you did not expect. He is a careful man of forty-five in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword, and he takes his hat off in your hall and holds it in both hands.

"My father is dying," he says. "He'd like to see it settled before he goes. So would I. I don't want it said I waited for him to die to bring it."

"You've brought it now."

"I've brought it to you first." He nods at your steward, who has gone grey in the face. "Your man's looking for a charter. I hope he finds it. I'd rather lose to a good charter than win against a bad one. It saves us both the next forty years."

"And if he doesn't find it?"

"Then I'll win," says Hervé, as if he is telling you the weather, "and we'll still have to live next to each other."

- Fight it at law, on the old charter. [Even]
- Buy him off. Name a sum and see if he is a man of business. (£30)
- Tell him a thing like this is better settled between families.

**You choose:** Fight it at law, on the old charter.

The steward finds a copy of a copy, in a hand nobody knows, with a hole where the witnesses should be. "It'll do," he says, in the voice of a man who knows it will not. Hervé's clerk asks, politely, whether he might see it, and makes a note.

### The First Michaelmas

*Autumn, year 11 of King Edwin*

Michaelmas, and the reckoning. The steward sets up his table in the hall with the counters and the cloth, and the reeves come in one by one with their tallies and their excuses, and the bailiffs behind them with the rents in leather bags.

You sit through all of it. Your father did not have a steward or a cloth or anything to count, and you still feel it, the weight of the bags, when they are put down in front of you. The rents are in, most of them. The men want paying. The roof of the long barn wants mending before the winter, and the smith wants his fifteen shillings, again.

"We can do what you want this year," says the steward. "We can't do all of it."

"We never could."

"No, my lord. But you used to be younger about it."

- Pay the household well, and let them know it was you. (£5)
- Put coin by against the suit, and against what comes after you.
- Lend to the neighbour who has come asking with his hat in his hands. (£10)

**You choose:** Pay the household well, and let them know it was you. (£5)

You pay every man his wage and a shilling over, and hand each one his money yourself. It takes until Vespers. Your hand aches. Old Hob in the stables bites his shilling, grins, and says, "Still good, then," and the whole yard laughs.

*Coin −£5*

### Penhoët's Offer

*Winter, year 11 of King Edwin*

A letter comes at Martinmas, in a shaking hand you have not seen for twenty years: Yann de Penhoët would like to see you before he dies, and will you come. It is an old house, older than yours by three hundred years, with its towers patched in newer stone and the boar over every door.

Yann is in bed in the great chamber with a fire built up like a forge. He is seventy, and he was a big man, and the bones are still big under the blanket.

"You," he says. "Look at you. Grey as me." He coughs for a while, and waves at the wine. "You stood against me at Lannec. I stood against you. We were both wrong, I expect. I'm too old to remember which way."

You pour him wine. He does not drink it.

"Kerval was my grandfather's," he says. "I'll die thinking it. Hervé will live thinking it. And one day his son will want it back, and your son will say no, and some boy will get his head broken over a mill." He looks at you. "I'd like it finished. My grandson Ronan is sixteen. You've a child of an age. Make a marriage, let the mill be the dowry, and let it be finished."

- Give him your hand on it. Robert for the Penhoët boy.
- Say you will put it to Robert before you answer.
- Tell him your children are not a price for a mill.

**You choose:** Say you will put it to Robert before you answer.

"Ask," says Yann, and laughs until he coughs. "My father never asked me. I'd have said no." At home Robert hears all of it, says he will think, and goes out to the stables to do it. By Candlemas he says yes, to your surprise and possibly to his own.

*Robert marries Béatrix de Penhoët*

### Old Penhoët

*Spring, year 12 of King Edwin*

Yann de Penhoët dies on Lady Day, with the bells going for the new year, which people at Penhoët say he would have liked, because he always did like to be noticed.

They bury him in the abbey church under a slab with the boar on it. Half the Armance comes, and stands in the cold nave in its best furs, and watches Hervé walk behind the coffin with his son Ronan at his shoulder. Hervé is head of Penhoët now. He looks exactly as he did yesterday, which is the thing about Hervé.

You stand with the family, as kin. Hervé gives you the place beside him and says nothing about it, which says a good deal.

- Pay for a Mass for the old man's soul, and say so.
- Watch Hervé, and the men who come to speak to him.
- Go home before the funeral feast.

**You choose:** Pay for a Mass for the old man's soul, and say so.

A pound to the abbey for a year of Masses. Hervé hears of it by the end of the week and sends you a cask of Penhoët wine, which is thin and sour and the best they have.

*Coin −£1; Sire Hervé de Penhoët's affection +1*

### The Old Company

*Summer, year 12 of King Edwin*

Hamon the farrier dies at haymaking, on his bench outside the forge, in the sun. He shod your horse the year you had one horse, and every horse after, and he would never take money from you for the first one.

His widow comes up to the hall in her good apron, with her sons behind her. "He'd want you told first," she says. "Before the priest. He said you'd want to know who was left."

You know who is left. You could count them on one hand now, and you do, under the table, while she talks.

- Pay for the burial, and carry the coffin yourself. (£2)
- Give the forge to his eldest, rent-free for his life.
- Send the widow money by the steward, and let the priest do the rest.

**You choose:** Pay for the burial, and carry the coffin yourself. (£2)

You take a corner of the coffin with Piers atte Brook and two of the old company, and your back tells you about it for a week. The whole village comes. At the grave somebody starts the story about Hamon and the Brésy horse, and somebody else corrects it, and by the end there is laughing in the churchyard, which the priest pretends not to hear.

*Coin −£2*

### The Summons

*Spring, year 13 of King Edwin*

The summons comes in Lent, sealed with the King's arms over the governor's own: the King's governor at Lannec would be glad of your attendance at Whitsun, to review the terms of your patent.

"Glad," says Piers, reading it over your shoulder. "That's a lawyer's word. It means you're to come whether you like it or not."

- Ride for Lannec.

**You choose:** Ride for Lannec.

### A Birth

- Name the child for a parent.

**You choose:** Name the child for a grandparent.

### The Patent

*Spring, year 13 of King Edwin*

You ride in at Whitsun with twenty men and a cushion for your saddle that you do not let anyone see.

The King's governor at Lannec is a lawyer from Wendmere with a fur collar and cold hands, and he has your patent on his table, unrolled and weighted at the corners with four of his own inkpots. He has gone through it with a pen, and there are marks in the margin of every grant.

"King Edwin is grateful," he says, "for your service. He would like it to be clear what the service was for. A patent is a contract. Contracts can be read more than one way. I would like us to agree on one."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the King's justices will read it for us." He smiles. "They are very thorough."

- Defend the patent, word for word. [Even]
- Concede a clause, and keep the King's goodwill.
- Pay his clerks to lose the question. (£20)

**You choose:** Defend the patent, word for word.

You take him through it clause by clause, as you once took a company through a ford, and at the end he has nothing in his margins that he can make stick. "The King will be pleased," he says, in a voice that means the King will be told. The West hears of it by Michaelmas.

### The Illness

*Winter, year 13 of King Edwin*

It comes at Christmas, with the hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there, and John asleep in a chair with his mouth open. Agnès is at the window, and turns when you move, and you see on her face what you look like.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse. You never thought about your body, except when it was bleeding, and now you think about nothing else.

"Well," says Piers atte Brook, sitting on the end of the bed. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"Thank you."

"There's a Sarenzan physician at Lannec who's cured a bishop. There's Saint Gildas's finger at the abbey. And there's lying still and doing what you're told, which you've never tried."

- Send to Lannec for the Sarenzan. (£10) [Even]
- Borrow Saint Gildas's finger from the abbey. [Even]
- Lie still and do as you are told, for once.

**You choose:** Send to Lannec for the Sarenzan. (£10)

The Sarenzan bleeds you, purges you, and charges you ten pounds. You are weaker after him than before. He says it is the disease leaving. Piers says it is ten pounds leaving.

*Coin −£10; Strength −1; Endurance −1*

### The Handover

*Spring, year 14 of King Edwin*

Lady Day. The new year, and the rents due, and the fields wanting their lord. You sit in the hall in your chair, with a fur over your knees, and you know.

You have known since the fever. You cannot hold the house another year. You can hold it from a chair, and you have seen men do that, and you have seen what happens to their houses while they do.

The household knows too. They have been looking at John for a month, the way haywards look at the sky. John has noticed, and has been careful not to notice. Agnès said last night, in the dark, "You'll have to do it properly, or they'll do it for you," and then pretended to sleep.

So you send for the steward, and the chaplain, and Piers atte Brook, and you think about how it is to be done.

- Before the whole household, in the hall, with your own hands.
- Quietly, by a letter to King Edwin, and let the household hear after.
- By a will with conditions, so your wishes bind after you.
- ~~Give it all up, and go into the abbey while you can still walk there.~~ (You are too weak to make the journey)

**You choose:** Before the whole household, in the hall, with your own hands.

You do it at dinner, with every soul in the house at the tables and the doors open to the yard. You stand, which takes some doing, and you take the ring off your finger and put it on John's, and say, "This is your lord now. Mind him." Somebody starts to cheer and stops. Then the whole hall is on its feet, and John stands there with your ring on and a face like milk.

**You choose:** Let the law stand.

## The Heir

> The son of a great man has two fathers: the one he had, and the one everybody else remembers.

### First Days

*Summer, year 14 of King Edwin*

The chair is too big. Nobody says so. It was made for your father, and the arms are worn pale where his hands went, and when you sit in it your own hands do not reach the same places.

Your father is upstairs. You can hear him cough through the floor at night, and in the day he sends down questions by the chamber servants: has the reeve been, what did the smith say, is the long barn roofed.

On the fourth morning Piers atte Brook brings you the first thing to sign. It is an order for the garrison: twelve men at the gate, day and night, as your father always had it. It costs as much as the long barn.

"Your father's way," Piers atte Brook says, and holds out the pen, and waits. So does the hall. You can feel it waiting, all the way to the kitchens.

- Sign it as it stands.
- Cut the gate to six men, roof the barn, and say why. [Favorable]
- Take it upstairs, and ask your father.

**You choose:** Cut the gate to six men, roof the barn, and say why.

You say it, and your voice goes up at the end like a boy's. The old man says, "As you like, my lord," and you would rather he had argued. The gate is cut to six. The men do it. In the yard you hear one of them say, "The old lord'd never," and the other one say, "Well, the old lord isn't here."

### The Will

*Autumn, year 14 of King Edwin*

After Michaelmas your father sends for the chaplain and for you, and dictates a will from the bed, a sentence at a time, with long stops for breath. You sit by the window and listen to your father give away your house.

Most of it is what you expected: the old company provided for, the Masses, the chaplain's horse. Then Robert. A manor of his own, the mill-farm at the ford and the land that goes with it, a third of what the house holds south of the river. And his Penhoët marriage to be kept, whatever comes.

It is generous. It is the kind of thing people do when they are dying and have a child they were hard on. It is a third of the land south of the river.

"Well?" says your father. His voice is thin. His eyes are not.

- Honour it, every line.
- Ask him to change it, to your face. [Even]
- Say nothing now, and change it when he is gone.

**You choose:** Ask him to change it, to your face.

You say it plainly: the house cannot carry it, and the house is what he built. Your father is angry, then quiet, then angry again. In the end he says, "Half, then. Half, and you tell them it was my idea." It is the closest to a compliment he has paid you since you were twelve.

### The Boundary Stone

*Winter, year 14 of King Edwin*

Candlemas, and frost, and a boy from the Kerval reeve at the gate before it is light, out of breath and white about the mouth.

Penhoët's men have been at the boundary in the night. They have taken up the stone by the mill-leat, the one your father showed you when you were small, and set it forty yards inside your land, and they are cutting the wood behind it now, with carts. It's a misunderstanding, Hervé's steward says, when you send to ask. A clerk's mistake about the line. He is sure his lord will be distressed.

The old knights are in the yard already, half-armed, waiting for somebody to tell them what to do. Upstairs your father is asleep, or pretending. Nobody has gone up. Everybody is looking at you.

- Arm, ride out, and put the stone back. [Even]
- Send to the court, and let the stone wait for the law. [Even]
- Let them have the wood. Keep the land.
- Ride to Penhoët, and speak to Hervé through Robert. [Even]

**You choose:** Arm, ride out, and put the stone back.

You ride out, and the Penhoët men do not stop. Somebody throws something. Somebody else draws. By the time it is over a Penhoët carter has a broken head and one of your grooms has lost two fingers, and the stone is still where they put it, and Hervé has a grievance he can take to any court in the West.

### The Deathbed

*Spring, year 15 of King Edwin*

Your father is dying at Lady Day, and knows it, and has the shutters open so he can hear the yard. The chaplain has been and gone. Agnès sits on the far side of the bed with her hands in her lap. The old company is on the stairs, taking turns.

He talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About the first manor, and the first winter, and the men who stayed.

Then he stops, and looks at you, and you can see him decide to say it.

"Look after Piers atte Brook; he'll not ask. And tell me. You've had a year of it." His hand finds yours. "Are you going to be me?"

- "I'll try to be."
- "No. I'm going to be me."
- Say nothing, and hold his hand.

**You choose:** "No. I'm going to be me."

Your father laughs, which hurts him, and grips your hand. "Good," he says. "Good. One of me was plenty." He dies two days later, without saying anything else about it.

*Hal Fletcher is dead*

### The Funeral

*Summer, year 15 of King Edwin*

They bury your father at midsummer, because the masons wanted the time and the West wanted to come.

It comes. A knight from Wendmere in the King's livery, sent by the governor, who stands at the front and looks at his boots. Mahaut sends her own steward with a pall of black and gold, and a letter in her own hand, which you do not open until after. Hervé de Penhoët walks with the family, as kin, with Robert beside him. The old company carries the coffin, all of them that can still carry, and two that cannot and do anyway.

Afterwards the chaplain reads the paragraph he has written for the house's book, in his best voice, with the whole hall listening:

"Hal Fletcher, head of the house from year 50 to year 53. He gave the house into his heir's hands while he still lived."

It is not enough. You did not expect it to be.

- Lay him in the chancel at home, under the floor he paid for.
- Carry him to Lannec, to lie among the great of the West. (£20)

**You choose:** Lay him in the chancel at home, under the floor he paid for.

Under the chancel floor, at the step, where the priest will walk over him every day and the village will see the stone every Sunday. The mason cuts the arms and the name and the year, and nothing else, because you could not decide what else, and in the end that seems right.

### The Oath

*Spring, year 16 of King Edwin*

Lady Day, year 55. You ride to Lannec to do homage for your father's lands, which are yours.

It is done in the governor's hall, before a new governor, a younger man with a sharper collar, who takes it for King Edwin with a clerk at his elbow writing down every word. You have watched your father do this. You have never done it. You go down on both knees on the cold stone, and put your hands between the hands held out to you, and the words are there in your mouth as if somebody else had put them in.

"I become your man," you say, "of the lands I hold of you, and faith I will bear to you of life and limb and earthly honour, against all men..."

And here you can stop, and say it plain. Or you can say more.

- Say the words plain, and nothing more.
- Offer a gift with your homage, to buy the goodwill your father had.
- Swear with a reservation, spoken aloud for the hall to hear. [Even]

**You choose:** Swear with a reservation, spoken aloud for the hall to hear.

"...saving the liberties of the West, and my right in Kerval." There is a silence. Then the governor says, "Noted," in a voice that takes the warmth out of the room, and the clerk writes it down. It is in the record now. So is the way it was received.

### The End

Here the prologue ends. You are head of the house Hal Fletcher founded, which is your house now, under King Edwin, with Kerval still in your hands and Penhoët still across the river.

## Prologue: The Old Lord

> A house is three generations: the man who makes it, the one who keeps it, and the one who spends it.

### The Hall

*Spring 912, the ninth year of Queen Mahaut*

The hall is cold in the mornings now, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the high table yet, and the steward stands at your elbow with the tallies under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

The crown of the West is eight years old, and Queen Mahaut wears it. You knelt to her at Lannec with the others, and she remembers who knelt first. You were a younger son of nobody in particular once. Now you hold Kerval, and men stand when you come in.

The family comes down to break their fast. Agnès takes the place on your right, as she has for years, and moves the salt so you can reach it. John sits below you, twenty, and eats like someone with somewhere to be. Robert sits beside him and steals from his trencher, the way he has since he could reach.

"The reeve's here about the lower field," says the steward. "And there's a man from Penhoët at the gate. He won't say what he wants. He says he'll wait."

### The Old Company

*Spring 912, the ninth year of Queen Mahaut*

Piers atte Brook comes up from the yard after Terce, the way he has every day since you had a yard. He has gone grey at the temples and bald on top, and he walks with one shoulder lower than the other, from a thing at a bridge you were both too young to be at.

"Your Penhoët's gone," he says. "Left a letter. I told him you'd read it when you'd eaten. He said he'd heard you were a man of appetite."

"And what did you say?"

"I said you'd heard he was a man of letters." He sits without asking, which nobody else in the hall would do, and pours himself your ale. "There's fewer of us at table every Christmas. You've noticed."

You have. You can count the old company on your hands now and have fingers over: the ones who rode with you when you had nothing, who held the line when holding it was stupid, who stood behind you at Lannec. The rest are names you say when the wine is good.

"I'm not asking for anything," says Piers. "I want that said first."

"Then you're the only man in the West who isn't."

"I didn't say I didn't want anything." He turns the cup. "I want to know what happens to us. After."

- Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.

**You choose:** Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.

"John?" He laughs. "He'll not want an old man telling him how you did it." "No," you say. "That's why." He thinks about it, and nods once, and finishes the ale like a man signing something.

### Penhoët's Suit

*Summer 912, the ninth year of Queen Mahaut*

The letter the Penhoët man left is a writ, and it is sealed with the Penhoët boar. Hervé de Penhoët sues you before Queen Mahaut's court at Lannec for Kerval, which, he says, was held by his grandfather and taken from his house without right.

He comes himself a week later, which you did not expect. He is a careful man of forty-five in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword, and he takes his hat off in your hall and holds it in both hands.

"My father is dying," he says. "He'd like to see it settled before he goes. So would I. I don't want it said I waited for him to die to bring it."

"You've brought it now."

"I've brought it to you first." He nods at your steward, who has gone grey in the face. "Your man's looking for a charter. I hope he finds it. I'd rather lose to a good charter than win against a bad one. It saves us both the next forty years."

"And if he doesn't find it?"

"Then I'll win," says Hervé, as if he is telling you the weather, "and we'll still have to live next to each other."

### The First Michaelmas

Michaelmas, and the reckoning. The steward sets up his table in the hall with the counters and the cloth, and the reeves come in one by one with their tallies and their excuses, and the bailiffs behind them with the rents in leather bags.

You sit through all of it. Your father did not have a steward or a cloth or anything to count, and you still feel it, the weight of the bags, when they are put down in front of you. The rents are in, most of them. The men want paying. The roof of the long barn wants mending before the winter, and the smith wants his fifteen shillings, again.

### Penhoët's Offer

*Winter 912, the ninth year of Queen Mahaut*

A letter comes at Martinmas, in a shaking hand you have not seen for twenty years: Yann de Penhoët would like to see you before he dies, and will you come. It is an old house, older than yours by three hundred years, with its towers patched in newer stone and the boar over every door.

Yann is in bed in the great chamber with a fire built up like a forge. He is seventy, and he was a big man, and the bones are still big under the blanket.

"You," he says. "Look at you. Grey as me." He coughs for a while, and waves at the wine. "You stood against me at Lannec. I stood against you. We were both wrong, I expect. I'm too old to remember which way."

You pour him wine. He does not drink it.

"Kerval was my grandfather's," he says. "I'll die thinking it. Hervé will live thinking it. You ride in at Whitsun with your banner and twenty men and a cushion for your saddle that you do not let anyone see.

### The Illness

*Winter 914, the eleventh year of Queen Mahaut*

It comes at Christmas, with the hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there, and John asleep in a chair with his mouth open. Agnès is at the window, and turns when you move, and you see on her face what you look like.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse. You never thought about your body, except when it was bleeding, and now you think about nothing else.

"Well," says Piers atte Brook, sitting on the end of the bed. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"Thank you."

"There's a Sarenzan physician at Lannec who's cured a bishop. There's Saint Gildas's finger at the abbey. And there's lying still and doing what you're told, which you've never tried."

## The Heir

> The son of a great man has two fathers: the one he had, and the one everybody else remembers.

### First Days

*Summer 915, the twelfth year of Queen Mahaut*

The chair is too big. Nobody says so. It was made for your father, and the arms are worn pale where his hands went, and when you sit in it your own hands do not reach the same places.

Your father is upstairs. You can hear him cough through the floor at night, and in the day he sends down questions by the chamber servants: has the reeve been, what did the smith say, is the long barn roofed.

On the fourth morning Piers atte Brook brings you the first thing to sign. It is an order for the garrison: twelve men at the gate, day and night, as your father always had it. It costs as much as the long barn.

"Your father's way," Piers atte Brook says, and holds out the pen, and waits. So does the hall. You can feel it waiting, all the way to the kitchens.

### The Will

*Autumn 915, the twelfth year of Queen Mahaut*

After Michaelmas your father sends for the chaplain and for you, and dictates a will from the bed, a sentence at a time, with long stops for breath. You sit by the window and listen to your father give away your house.

Most of it is what you expected: the old company provided for, the Masses, the chaplain's horse. Then Robert. A manor of his own, the mill-farm at the ford and the land that goes with it, a third of what the house holds south of the river. And his Penhoët marriage to be kept, whatever comes.

It is generous. It is the kind of thing people do when they are dying and have a child they were hard on. It is a third of the land south of the river.

"Well?" says your father. His voice is thin. His eyes are not.

**You choose:** Ask him to change it, to your face.

You say it plainly: the house cannot carry it, and the house is what he built. Your father is angry, then quiet, then angry again. In the end he says, "Half, then. Half, and you tell them it was my idea." It is the closest to a compliment he has paid you since you were twelve.

### The Deathbed

*Spring 916, the thirteenth year of Queen Mahaut*

Your father is dying at Lady Day, and knows it, and has the shutters open so he can hear the yard. The chaplain has been and gone. Agnès sits on the far side of the bed with her hands in her lap. The old company is on the stairs, taking turns.

He talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About the first manor, and the first winter, and the men who stayed.

## Book One: The Keeper

### The Homage of the Knights

*Summer 917, the fourteenth year of Queen Mahaut*

They come at midsummer, as they came to your father every year: the knights who hold land of the house, riding in by twos and threes with their squires and their best horses and their oldest grievances. Sir Gautier d'Aubrac, Sir Alain de Coatmen and Sir Renaud de Saint-Aubin, and the rest behind them.

The hall is hung with the house's colours. The chair at the end of it is yours. You sit in it with your hands on the arms where your father's hands went, and they come up one by one, and kneel, and put their hands between yours.

"I become your man of the lands I hold of you," says the first, an old knight with a white beard and a voice like a cart on gravel, "as I was your father's." He says the last part quietly, as if it is between the two of you.

### Old Knights, New Men

The ship's master, between cups, says there is a sickness in Sarenza this spring, in the poor quarters by the harbour. "Takes the children first," he says. "Like the Mottle, but backwards." He drinks. "Nothing to do with us."

- Keep male preference. Sons first, then daughters.
- Adopt the male line. Only sons, and only through sons.
- Divide the lands among the sons when the time comes.
- Name your heir by will, whatever the law says.

**You choose:** Keep male preference. Sons first, then daughters.

The house's law stays as your father kept it.

*The house's law: male preference*

## Book One, Act Three: The Children's Mortality

> In the second Mottle the old buried the young, which is the wrong way round.

### The Sickness Comes

*Summer 925, the twenty-second year of Queen Mahaut*

The ship's master's sickness in Sarenza is in Sauvemer by Midsummer, in the harbour quarter first, in the children first. By Lammas it is in Lannec. By the Assumption a rider comes up the valley to Kerval with his face wrapped, and does not get off his horse to say that it is in the next village, a day's ride off.

Azenor is in the nursery with the children. The steward is at the gate. Your father lived through the first Mottle, and used to say that the people who lived were the ones who were somewhere else.

- Shut the gates of the hall and the village. Nobody in, nobody out.
- Stay with the people. Open the hall to the sick.
- Take the children up into the hills, to the shepherds' farms, until it passes.

**You choose:** Stay with the people. Open the hall to the sick.

The hall is a hospital by Michaelmas, with straw on the floor and the priest going down the rows. You walk through it every morning. The valley will never forget it.

*Kerval: the village warmer; Standing with the commons: much higher; Piety: higher; Coin −£10; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and slack trade: £227 8s 4d came in; the household £22 14s 10d, the men's pay £8 2s and repairs £20 went out; The sickness in Kerval: 29 dead this year*

### The Sealed Village

*Autumn 925, the twenty-second year of Queen Mahaut*

At Michaelmas the reeve of the outlying farms comes to the boundary stone and shouts across it: the sickness is in his hamlet, in four houses, and he asks whether to seal it, nobody in, nobody out, the way they did in the first Mottle.

- Seal it, and send food to the boundary stone every week. (£10)
- Seal it, and send nothing.
- Leave it open. You cannot keep the sick from their own fields.

**You choose:** Seal it, and send food to the boundary stone every week. (£10)

The hamlet is sealed with a rope across the lane and a cross on a post. The carts go to the stone every Monday. Half the hamlet lives, which in that year is a great deal.

*Coin −£10; Grain in store (seasons) -1; -6 people at Kerval*

### The Crown in the Plague

*Spring 926, the twenty-third year of Queen Mahaut*

Queen Mahaut is shut in Lannec, and has been since Lammas. The Estates do not meet. The courts do not sit. The West is governed, if it is governed, by whoever holds its valleys, and the house holds more of them than most.

- Govern your own country. Justice, the market, the roads.
- Send grain and men to Lannec.

**You choose:** Govern your own country. Justice, the market, the roads.

You hold the house's court in the open air at the cross-roads, under an awning, with the litigants twenty paces off. The valley has more justice that year than it has had since the war.

*The household: more loyal; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and steady trade: £235 16s 8d came in; the household £23 11s 8d, the men's pay £8 2s and repairs £20 went out; The sickness in Kerval: 27 dead this year*

### Black on Penhoët's Gate

*Autumn 926, the twenty-third year of Queen Mahaut*

At Michaelmas the Mottle takes Ronan de Penhoët, at thirty, in three days, the way it takes the young. Hervé survives him, at fifty-nine, and stands at the grave, they say, for an hour after the priest has gone. Penhoët's heir is Ronan's child, a boy of six.

There is black on Penhoët's gate by Martinmas, and the Penhoët bell rings every evening for a month.

- Send condolence, and pay for a Mass for Ronan's soul.
- Send a man to count what Penhoët has left.
- Send nothing.

**You choose:** Send a man to count what Penhoët has left.

Your man comes back at Martinmas: half the tenants dead, a third of the knights, the harvest standing in the fields for want of hands, and the boundary at Kerval unwatched.

### Succour or Strike

*Winter 926, the twenty-third year of Queen Mahaut*

In the dead of winter Penhoët is as weak as it has been in a hundred years. Its men are dead or sick, its harvest is rotting in the fields, and its boundary at Kerval has nobody on it. You know exactly how weak; your man counted.

The steward says that a house at its weakest remembers who came to its door, both ways.

- Send grain, and men to bring in Penhoët's harvest. [Even]
- Strike while it is weak. Take back what Penhoët holds on the Kerval side of the stream. [Even]
- Leave it. Penhoët will recover.

**You choose:** Send grain, and men to bring in Penhoët's harvest.

Hervé meets the carts at the gate and sends them back. Penhoët has never needed charity, he says, and will not start in a plague year. Pride is the last thing a house loses.

*Penhoët: colder*

### The Company at the Gate

*Spring 927, the twenty-fourth year of Queen Mahaut*

In the spring of 927 a free company comes down the valley road, eighty men who have been living on the plague country since the March war. Their captain rides to the gate under a flag of truce and offers the house his protection for the summer, at a price.

- Pay them, and send them on. (£15)
- Fight them, with the house's men. [Risky]

**You choose:** Pay them, and send them on. (£15)

Fifteen pounds, and they ride on over the hills. The valley is safe for the year, and knows what it cost.

*Coin −£15*

### The Friar

*Summer 927, the twenty-fourth year of Queen Mahaut*

A friar comes through in the summer with forty people behind him, barefoot, singing, beating their own backs with knotted cords until the blood runs. The Mottle, he preaches at the cross, is God's anger at the lords, who have taken the poor man's sons for their wars and his rents for their wine. The crowd listens. So does your steward.

- Let him preach, and give his people bread.
- Hand him to the bishop's men.

**You choose:** Let him preach, and give his people bread.

He preaches for three days and moves on, and takes eleven of your people with him, and the valley talks about the lords' wars and the poor man's sons all winter.

*Standing with the commons: higher; Standing with the Church: lower; Grain in store (seasons) -1; -11 people at Kerval; Michaelmas, a failed harvest and brisk trade: £149 15s came in; the household £14 19s 6d, the men's pay £8 2s and repairs £20 went out; The sickness in Kerval: 25 dead this year; Sir Hervé de Morlaix withholds his dues this year, over a quarrel the house's court will have to hear*

### The Count of the Dead

*Spring 928, the twenty-fifth year of Queen Mahaut*

At Lady Day in 928 the sickness lifts, all at once, the way the first one did, for no reason anyone can give. The bell stops. The pit behind the church is filled in and sown with grass.

The steward brings the roll of who is left: the household, the villages, the family. The valley does not forget who walked the rows in the hall. Piers atte Brook, who rode with your father from the beginning, is on the roll with a cross. He was the last of the old company.

At the gate there is a child, a cousin of the house, eight years old, whose parents are in the pits at Sauvemer.

- Take the child in, as a ward of the house.
- Send the child to the Church.
- Find the child a place with one of the house's knights.

**You choose:** Take the child in, as a ward of the house.

The child is given a bed in the nursery and a place at the end of the table, and on the first night asks whether you are going to die too. You say not this year.

*The household: more loyal*

### The Keeper's Will

*Summer 928, the twenty-fifth year of Queen Mahaut*

At midsummer you write the will your father wrote, with the priest and the steward, at the same table. You are thirty-six. You have children living, and the stone in the chancel has names on it that it did not have three years ago. You write it your own way, and the steward does not read you your father's first.

- Name your heir by will, and seal it.
- Name a guardian for a minority: Robert.
- Provide for the younger ones, out of the lands.

**You choose:** Name your heir by will, and seal it.

You write the name, and seal it, and give it to the priest. Whatever the law says, the will says this, and there will be a quarrel at your graveside if they ever disagree.

### The End

Here Act III of Book One ends. The Mottle is over. You have children living, and a will that names one of them. The house is a great house.

Act IV, The Test of the Law, is still being written.

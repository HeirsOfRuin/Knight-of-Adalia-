# The Mercer

Background: Burgess's son. Seed 9.

## The Counting-House

*Spring, year 9 of King Aldred*

Wendham, the week after Easter. Fleeces stacked to the rafters, and the whole house smelling of grease and wet wool.

A Sarenzan in a black gown comes to your father's counting-house with a clerk and a locked box. Master Bertuccio Lanzi. He bows to your mother as if she were a countess, and your father sends you out of the room before the door is shut.

The door does not quite shut. Through the crack you hear figures you do not understand and one word you do: owed.

In the yard, Jocelin Tanner and the tanners' boys are going down to the wharf to throw stones at the Sarenzan ship. Your father's clerk is at his desk by the stair, doing sums for nobody.

- Stay by the door and listen.
- Go to the wharf with Jocelin and the tanners' boys.
- Sit with the clerk and learn his sums.

**Chose:** Sit with the clerk and learn his sums.

He shows you the abacus and the way to reckon a sack of wool in Sarenzan money and in Adalian, and the difference between the two, which is where the profit lives. By evening you can do it faster than he can. He does not like that much.

*Trade +1 · Ralf Mercer's affection +1*

## The Wet Year

*Summer, year 11 of King Aldred*

You are 10.

It has rained since Whitsun. The barley lies flat in the fields and sprouts in the ear. Sheep cough in the folds. By Lammas, bread costs three times what it did, and the bread is half bean flour and grit.

In Wendham the merchants are doing well. Grain comes in on Sarenzan ships and sells for whatever the seller says. In the tanners' alley behind your house, children are eating nettles.

- Carry bread from your mother's kitchen to the tanners' alley.
- Set snares in Lord Ravell's chase after dark. *(Even)*
- Count grain for Master Lanzi's ship, buying cheap from farmers who must sell.
- Get into the tithe barn at night and fill a sack. *(Even)*

**Chose:** Count grain for Master Lanzi's ship, buying cheap from farmers who must sell.

Master Lanzi pays you a shilling and remembers your name. The farmers' carts go home empty.

## What a Boy Learns

*Summer, year 12 of King Aldred*

You are 11. The harvest came in at last, thin but enough.

A boy of your sort has perhaps four years before he is a man and the shape of his life is set. There is time to get good at one thing, if someone will teach it.

The guild keeps a schoolmaster for members' sons. There is a fencing master by the bridge who teaches apprentices to use a buckler, against the town's rules.

Whatever you choose, something else goes untaught.

- Learn your letters, Latin and Adalian, properly.
- Learn to fight with sword and buckler.
- Learn how gentlefolk eat, speak and stand, by serving at their tables.
- Learn horses and hounds, riding and the chase.

**Chose:** Learn how gentlefolk eat, speak and stand, by serving at their tables.

You learn to carve a capon in the proper joints, pour without spilling, kneel on the correct knee, and never speak first. It feels like learning to act a part. You suspect that is what it is.

*Courtesy +2*

## Fourteen

*Autumn, year 15 of King Aldred*

You are 14.

Fourteen is old enough to be bound apprentice, or put to the plough, or sent for a soldier. It is the age when a boy of your sort is either taken into somebody's service or left where he was born.

Your father has decided you will not be a wool merchant. He wants a son in a knight's household, and he has money to spend on it, though not much.

**Chose:** Go and see what can be made of it.

## A Place Bought or Begged

Your father has done the sums. A place in a knight's household costs money, or favour, or both. He has a little of each.

Sir Hamon Darrell owes the Wendham wool merchants six pounds for fleeces he sold twice. He needs a squire and cannot afford one. Master Osbert Fuller, warden of the guild, dines with Lord Ravell's steward once a year, and this year it is Thursday. And there is your own purse, which you have kept since you were eight. It holds £1 1s.

Your mother says you are too good for knights. Your father tells her that is the point.

- Pay Sir Hamon's price yourself, a pound down and the rest from your father.
- Help your father put it to Sir Hamon. His debt forgiven, for your place. *(Favorable)*
- Ask Master Osbert Fuller to speak for you to Lord Ravell's steward. *(Even)*
- Tell your father you will make your own way, and wait.

**Chose:** Pay Sir Hamon's price yourself, a pound down and the rest from your father.

Sir Hamon takes the money the way gentlemen take money, without looking at it. Your father finds the other five pounds from somewhere. Later you learn where. It was the Lanzi bank, against next year's clip.

## Leaving

*Spring, year 16 of King Aldred*

You are 15, and you leave in the morning.

You go as a knight's boy, which is not yet a squire and is a long way from a knight. You will carry, clean, fetch, serve, and be hit.

Your father gives you a purse and a list of the people in Lord Ravell's country who owe the family money. Your mother gives you three shirts. Annot Dyer is at the dye-vats by the river, her arms blue to the elbow. She stops working when you come down the lane.

- Go to Annot and tell her you will come back.
- Promise your father a share of whatever you earn, sent home every quarter.
- Leave before dawn, before anyone can make you promise anything.

**Chose:** Promise your father a share of whatever you earn, sent home every quarter.

He writes it down. He is a merchant. You walk past the girl without stopping. You tell yourself you will write.

## Ravell Hall

You are 15, and you have never seen so many people in one place who were not at a fair.

You see Ravell Hall first from the ridge road, a mile off, across the water meadows: a square grey keep with a timber hall built against its flank like a barn leaning on a church, a curtain wall, a gatehouse, a moat that catches the light. Smoke from a dozen chimneys. Around the walls, outside them, the village: the mill, the smithy, the church of Saint Ebba with its squat tower, the alehouse with a bush over the door. Strip fields running down to the river. Sheep everywhere.

Up close it smells. The moat is green and takes the castle's slops, and in spring it ripens. The gate passage is dark and wet and full of carts. And then you are through, into the outer ward, and it is like being dropped into a hive.

Grooms leading horses two at a time. A cartload of firewood being unloaded by men who swear at it. Laundresses carrying baskets the size of coffins. A falconer with a hooded bird on his fist, walking slowly so as not to unsettle it. Two boys in good cloth fighting with wooden swords while a grey old knight, missing two fingers on his left hand, tells them both they are dead. Hounds. Chickens. A priest. A man in a fur-trimmed gown shouting at a man in a plain one about candles. Nobody looks at you, because everyone here is going somewhere and you are not yet anyone.

*(Continue)*

Lord Thurstan Ravell holds this castle and eleven manors from the King. He holds four knights' fees under him: four knights who owe him forty days' service a year, mounted and armed, in return for their land. Sir Hamon Darrell of Ashby and Sir Ancel Brome of Brome Tower are two of those four. Their households live under his roof for half the year, eat his bread, and fight his quarrels. Yours will too.

Sir Hamon meets you at the foot of the hall steps with a cup of ale in his hand, his belt let out a notch, and two of his hounds leaning against his legs. He is a big man going soft, with a broad red face, white hair cut short, and the kind of smile that has never once in his life been calculated. "There he is," he says, to nobody in particular, and then to you, "You'll have eaten? No. Of course not. Nobody feeds anybody on the road." He shouts for bread. It comes. He watches you eat it as if he had made it himself.

*(Continue)*

You will sleep in the squires' loft over the great chamber, with four others, on straw pallets under a roof that drips in one corner. The loft has its own rules, and nobody tells them to you; you learn them by breaking them.

The eldest of the four is Giles Marrick, nineteen, the second son of a Carrow knight, with good teeth and a better sword and a way of smiling at the side of your head instead of your face. He looks at your boots, which are your father's, then your hands, which are not a gentleman's, then your face, and decides something. You watch him decide it.

Aymer Ravell is there too: the lord's younger son, sixteen, fair, quick, restless, already bored with you and everyone else. He asks where you are from, and when you tell him he says "Ah," as if that explained something, and goes back to his dice.

That night you lie awake listening to the castle: a dog barking in the outer ward, the watch calling the hours, someone singing badly in the hall below, a woman laughing somewhere, rain on the roof. You think about home. You think about how far down you are, here, and how many people stand between you and anything. You count them, because counting is what you do. You lose count somewhere past forty.

- Kneel to your master in the yard, properly, as if you had been doing it all your life. *(Even)*
- Go straight to the stables and make yourself useful before anyone tells you to.
- Find someone in the hall who might tell you how things are done here.

**Chose:** Kneel to your master in the yard, properly, as if you had been doing it all your life. (Even: failure)

You go down on one knee in the yard, and the knee goes straight into a puddle that is not entirely water. The words come out in the wrong order. You say "faithfully" twice.

Somebody in the squires' window above laughs, a high, delighted laugh. You do not look up. You know already whose it will be.

Your master tells you to get up. Sir Hamon is kind about it, which makes it worse: he pats your shoulder and says that he knelt in a cowpat on the day he was knighted and it never did him any harm. You spend the evening scrubbing your hose.

*Giles Marrick's respect −1*

## Winter at the Pell

*Winter, year 16 of King Aldred*

Snow on the moat, ice on the trough, and the hall full of smoke from Martinmas to Candlemas.

Winter is when a household learns. Sir Bertram Oakes takes the young men to the pell every morning before Mass, whatever the weather. Master Ranulf breaks the new horses in the covered ride. Father Benet keeps the lord's accounts and letters in a cold room over the chapel, and will let a boy sit in if he holds his tongue. Lady Ravell keeps her own hall, where young men learn to be tolerable company or are not invited back.

You have the winter. You cannot be everywhere.

- Sir Bertram's pell, every morning, until your arms stop shaking.
- The covered ride with Master Ranulf and the young horses.

- Lady Ravell's hall, where the young men learn to be company.

**Chose:** Father Benet's cold room, and the lord's accounts.

You learn how a great household is really run: by debt, by delay, and by a clerk who knows where everything went.

## Sir Hamon's Debts

*Summer, year 17 of King Aldred*

Sir Hamon owes money. Everyone in the household knows it except Sir Hamon, who knows it the way a man knows he will die: in principle, not today.

This summer it becomes today. Master Bertuccio Lanzi of the Lanzi bank rides in from Wendham with a clerk and a letter. Forty pounds, borrowed against Ashby's wool. And there is the King's subsidy: Sir Hamon was named collector for the hundred last year. He collected it. He has not yet paid it over. When he looks for it, it is not all there.

He calls you in. He is not angry. He is frightened, which is worse. "You can count," he says. "Count this."

- Go down to Master Lanzi yourself and talk him into waiting. *(Favorable)*

**Chose:** Go down to Master Lanzi yourself and talk him into waiting. (Favorable: failure)

The Wendham burgesses have a table below the salt, with their wives and daughters. Master Osbert Fuller's daughter Cecily is the one doing her father's talking for him.

## The Leven Ford

Dawn on the fourth day, in a cold mist off the river. Black Ewan's men are driving the cattle across the Leven ford, slowly, because cattle will not be hurried through water. Eighty men, perhaps more, half of them on the far bank already. They have not seen you.

Sir Ancel draws up the horse behind a fold of ground. Will Cobb has the archers strung and waiting in the alders. Sir Hamon sits the Ravell banner and looks old in the grey light. Beside him is Sir Hugh Wyck, a knight of Lord Ravell's from the next valley, who smiles at you as if this were a hunt.

Sir Ancel says the horse will go in when the archers have loosed twice. That is all the plan there is.

You have no armour. A ford fight is a lottery for a man without it.

- Take two of Cobb's men upstream to the second ford and get behind the herd. *(Risky)*

## The King at Ravell

You are 18.

## Sauvemer

Hautbois won the King a battle. It did not win him a port, and without a port on this coast the army goes home and Valdrenne forgets it was ever here.

*(Continue)*

## The Useless Mouths

*Summer, year 22 of King Aldred*

By summer Sauvemer is eating its horses.

One morning the land gate opens and five hundred people come out: old men, women, children, the sick. The castellan has put out everyone who cannot fight, to save the bread for those who can. They come across the ditch toward the Adalian lines with their hands up.

The King's order comes down at once. Nobody is to pass. If the town wants to feed them, it can take them back. If not, they can stay where they are, between the walls and the lines, and the town can watch.

*(Continue)*

On the third day a woman comes out of the land gate alone, under a white cloth, with a basket: the castellan's wife, Dame Clémence de Vaux, with what bread she can carry. ## The Relief That Did Not Come

At the end of July, King Amaury comes at last. His army appears on the heights above the marshes: banners, tents, the whole great host of Valdrenne, rebuilt since Hautbois.

## The Garrison

*Autumn, year 23 of King Aldred*

A truce is a strange thing to live in. You are a conqueror in a conquered country, which sounds grand. In fact it is boredom, cold, short pay, and the feeling of being watched.

The Sauvemer people have not forgiven the siege and will not, in your lifetime. The Adalian garrison drinks, gambles, marries local girls who would rather not, quarrels with the townsmen, and sometimes kills one. The townsmen sometimes kill one back. Every month there is a hanging.

## Prologue: The Old Lord

### The Crown of the West

*Spring 912, the seventh year of King Plan*

You have worn the crown of the West since the oaths at Lannec. It is heavier than it looked.

**You choose:** Go on.

### The King at Lannec

*Spring 912, the seventh year of King Plan*

The great hall at Lannec is cold in the mornings, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the dais yet, and the chamberlain stands at your elbow with the day's petitions under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

You were a wool merchant's son once. Now the hall stands when you come in, and the hall is the hall where the Estates of the West made you king, and every morning you walk past the place where you stood that day and do not look at it.

Mahaut comes down with her women. She is thirty-one, and Queen of the West, and Duchess of Armance in her own right, which she has never once let a clerk forget. She takes the chair beside yours and moves the salt so you can reach it. Jehanne comes after her at a run, six years old, in a gown she has already torn, and climbs into your lap without asking, as she has since she could climb. The younger ones come behind with their nurse, in order of size.

"Your daughter," says Mahaut, "has been in the stables again."

"My daughter," you say, "is going to be Queen of the West. She can be in the stables if she likes."

"That," says Mahaut, "is what I am afraid of."

- Take Jehanne up on your saddle and ride out to the fields.
- Go through the treasurer's rolls before the petitioners come.
- Hear the petitions in the great hall yourself.

**You choose:** Take Jehanne up on your saddle and ride out to the fields.

You ride out by the river gate with Jehanne in front of you on the saddle, holding the reins she thinks are steering the horse. The people on the road stop and bow and stare. "Why are they looking?" she asks. "Because you're going to be their queen." She thinks about that for a furlong. "Can I have a horse of my own, then?"

### The Old Company

Davy Ludd comes up from the guardroom after Terce, the way he has every day since there was a guardroom to come up from. He is grey now, and walks with one shoulder lower than the other, and he does not bow to the King of the West unless there is somebody watching.

### Quérec

*Summer 912, the seventh year of King Plan*

The summer court ends with the oaths, as it does every year: the lords of the West renew their faith to the King, and this year, for the first time, you have asked them to swear to Jehanne as your heir.

They swear, one after another, and kiss the child's small hand, and she bears it with a face like a bishop. Then the lord of Quérec comes forward, and stands, and does not kneel.

He is old now, the salt lord of the southern marsh, the last man in the West who still says what he thinks of you to your face.

"I swore to you," Quérec says, loud enough for the hall. "I'll keep it. ### The Exchequer

*Autumn 912, the seventh year of King Plan*

Michaelmas, and the crown's reckoning. The treasurer sets up his chequered cloth in the lower hall, and the sheriffs of the West come in one by one to account for their shires, with their tallies and their excuses, and the clerks move the counters on the squares like a game nobody wins.

You sit through all of it, as you always have. The salt penny is in. The Sauvemer customs are short, again. The castles on the march want mending, the fleet wants paying, and the Sarenzans want their interest, politely, by letter, in a hand you have learned to dread.

### The Embassy

*Winter 912, the seventh year of King Plan*

At Martinmas an embassy comes over the hills from Cordelle with forty horses and a bishop, to ask for the heir of the West.

King Amaury VII offers his brother, Prince Lothaire, twenty, for Jehanne. A betrothal now, a marriage when the children are of age, and peace on the march for as long as it lasts. The bishop puts it beautifully. The Valdrennish lords stand behind him and look at the hall as if pricing it.

Mahaut listens to all of it without moving. When they have gone to their lodgings she says, "Amaury tried to marry me to that boy when he was eleven. Now he wants my daughter for him." She turns her rings. "My grandmother let me choose. I chose you."

The peace with Valdrenne is a paper and a habit. Amaury is offering to make it a family.

You send them home with gifts and a refusal so courteous that it takes the bishop a day's ride to understand it. On the march that summer the raiders are bolder. Mahaut, at supper, puts her hand over yours on the table, and leaves it there.

### The Old Company

*Spring 913, the eighth year of King Plan*

Hamon the farrier dies at haymaking, on his bench outside the forge, in the sun. He shod your horse the year you had one horse, and every horse after, and he would never take money from you for the first one.

*Spring 914, the ninth year of King Plan*

You call the Estates of the West to Lannec at Whitsun in 914, and they come: the lords on their benches, the bishops in their stalls, the burgesses of the salt towns at the back in their good wool, all of them in the hall where they made you king.

### The Illness

*Winter 914, the ninth year of King Plan*

It comes at Christmas, with the great hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there. Mahaut is sitting beside it in her furs with a book of accounts on her knee, and turns a page, and looks up when you move, and you see on her face what you look like. Jehanne is asleep at the foot of the bed in a nest of cloaks, with her shoes on.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse.

"Well," says Davy, from the door. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"There is a Sarenzan physician in the town," says Mahaut, "who cured a bishop. There is the relic of Saint Gildas at the abbey. And there is lying still and doing what you're told." She closes the book. "I would choose the third. You won't."

- Have the saint's finger brought from the abbey. [Even]

**You choose:** Lie still and do as Mahaut says, for once.

You lie still. Mahaut runs the kingdom from a chair beside your bed for the whole of Lent, and runs it well, and tells you about it every evening whether you want to hear or not. It is the first winter in thirty years you have not ridden out. The West, you notice, does not fall down.

### The Crowning

*Spring 915, the tenth year of King Plan*

Lady Day, the new year. You sit in the gallery in a chair with a fur over your knees, and you know.

You will live. The physicians say so, and you believe them, and you can feel it: you will sit in the sun for years yet. But you will not ride to the march again, or sit a whole day's assize, or hold a great council by the force of standing up in it. The kingdom cannot wait for you to get better. It can only wait for Jehanne to grow up.

The kings of Valdrenne used to do it this way, in the old days, when they were afraid: crown the heir while the father lived, so that there was never a day without a crowned head. Jehanne is nine. Her brothers and sisters are younger. Mahaut would govern for her. You would be there, behind the throne, for as long as you last.

The Estates swore to "the King's heir, as the law shall be". That will not help. Bishop Évrard of Saint-Lys, who crowned you, is a hundred and one, and has sent word that he would like to crown one more, if it is soon.

- Crown her at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.
- Crown her quietly in the palace chapel, and tell the West after.
- Crown her, and name her council in the same hour, so no one else can.

**You choose:** Crown her at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.

They carry you into the cathedral in a chair, and you stand for the crowning, because you will not sit for it. Old Évrard sets a small crown on Jehanne's head with hands that shake so badly two canons have to steady them, and the oldest man in the West crowns the youngest, and the nave roars. Jehanne does not cry, and does not smile, and does not drop the sceptre, which is heavier than she is. Afterwards, in the sacristy, she is sick into a basin, and then asks for her dinner.

## The Heir

> Woe to the land whose king is a child, said the preacher. He had not met this one.

### The Council

*Summer 915, the first year of Queen Jehanne*

You are nine years old, and Queen of the West, and the chair in the council chamber was made for your father. Your feet do not reach the floor. Somebody has put a footstool under them, tactfully, and you would like to kick it across the room.

Your mother sits at the other end of the table, and governs. She is the regent. That is a word you have learned this spring, along with "minority", "the Great Seal", and "Your Grace", which is what the chancellor calls you now, every time, as if checking it still works.

Your father is in his rooms along the gallery. He is better. He sits in the sun in the mornings, and you go and see him after council, and he asks you what they said, and laughs.

Today the council is talking about Quérec. He swore to you, and kissed your hand, and has sent a letter congratulating your mother on her regency. The council thinks that is worse. They talk across you, the way grown men talk across a child at dinner. Nobody asks you anything.

- Ask a question, out loud, in the middle of it. [Favorable]
- Say nothing, and count who agrees with whom.
- Go to your father after, and ask him what to say.

**You choose:** Ask a question, out loud, in the middle of it.

"What does Quérec want?" you say. It comes out loud. The council stops. The chancellor opens his mouth and shuts it. Your mother looks at you for a long moment, and then says, "That is the question, my lords. Answer the Queen." They try. None of them knows. You see your mother see that.

### The Regency

*Autumn 915, the first year of Queen Jehanne*

After Michaelmas your mother sends for you to her own chamber, with only her old nurse present, the way she once sent for your father when they were deciding something nobody could know yet.

"The council," she says, "is your father's men, and my men, and they hate each other quietly, which is the best kind of council. I want to change it." She puts a list in front of you. Half the names on it are Armance: her people, her cousins, her grandmother's old captains. "These men held the Armance for a woman through a war. Your father's men put a crown on a man. I think you need the first kind more."

"Why are you asking me?" you say.

"Because you are the Queen," she says, "and one day you will remember who I asked."

- Give her the Armance council she wants.
- Keep your father's men. They made the crown.
- Take half her names, and keep half his. [Favorable]

**You choose:** Take half her names, and keep half his.

You go through the list with her, name by name, and keep the ones who have been to the march and the marsh, and lose the ones who only talk. She argues. You argue back. At the end she sits back and looks at you and says, "Your father would have kept all his," and you cannot tell whether she is pleased.

### The Marsh

*Winter 915, the first year of Queen Jehanne*

At Candlemas the oath Quérec swore in the great hall, with your father's guard along the wall, turns out to have been worth what such oaths are worth. He takes the crown's salt-works at the edge of the marsh, and the toll bridge, and says in a letter to every lord in the West that he swore to a King, not to a child and her mother.

Forty marsh lords are with him, and two hundred men, and a ship from Valdrenne has been seen off the salt flats, though nobody can say whose.

The council meets for three days. The constable wants to march at once, in the frost. The chancellor wants to treat. Your mother listens to both and says nothing, which you have learned means she has decided and is waiting to see if anyone agrees.

On the third day she turns to you. "It is your crown he has hung on that bridge," she says. "What do you want?"

Quérec holds the tower for two days, and then asks for terms. Your mother gives him the terms, in your name, and makes you watch her do it.

### The King's Question

*Spring 916, the second year of Queen Jehanne*

In the spring your father is well enough to sit in the garden at the foot of the palace wall, in the sun, with a blanket and a cup of wine he is not supposed to have.

You sit with him most mornings, after council. Today he talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from: a wool merchant's son, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About your mother, the garden at Lannec, the hawk on her fist that she did not need. About the war, and the bridge, and the Estates, and the morning they made him king and he stood in the hall and could not feel his own feet.

Then he stops, and looks at you, and you can see him decide to say it.

"You're ten," he says. "You've had a year of it. Bridges and councils and your mother." He takes your hand. His is dry and light. "Tell me. Are you going to be me?"

He laughs, which hurts him, and grips your hand. "Good," he says. "Good. One of me was plenty."

### The Bishop of Saint-Lys

*Summer 916, the second year of Queen Jehanne*

Bishop Évrard of Saint-Lys dies at midsummer, at a hundred and two, in the middle of the Office, between one psalm and the next. He crowned your father. He crowned you. ### The Acclamation

*Spring 917, the third year of Queen Jehanne*

Lady Day, 917. You are eleven. The Estates of the West meet at Lannec to acclaim you, in person, as their Queen: not as a child with a crown on, but as the Queen they will obey.

The hall is full. Your father sits at the side of the dais in a chair with arms, where everyone can see him and nobody can say he is ruling. Your mother stands behind your chair, which is the old King's chair, with the footstool gone, because you have grown. Quérec is on the marsh lords' bench, under guard, and will kneel today whether he likes it or not.

The chancellor has written you a speech. It is good, and long, and in Latin at the end. Your mother has read it and said nothing, which means she does not like it. Your father has read it and said, "Say what you like. They'll remember that."

The hall goes quiet. It is your turn.

- Say the speech the chancellor wrote, every word.
- Put the speech down, and say something of your own. [Favorable]
- Let your mother speak for you.

**You choose:** Put the speech down, and say something of your own.

"I am eleven," you say. "My father was nobody, and my mother's grandmother held the Armance against a king. I don't know which of them I'll be. You'll find out when I do." There is a silence like a held breath. Then your father laughs out loud from his chair, and the hall laughs with him, and then it roars. Your mother puts her hand on your shoulder from behind, and leaves it there.

## Book One: The Keeper

### The Homage of the Lords

*Summer 917, the third year of Queen Jehanne*

At midsummer the great lords of the West come to Lannec to do you homage in person, now that the Estates have acclaimed you: every man who holds of the crown, one by one, on his knees, with his hands between yours.

Your hands are small. The lords' hands are not. By the twentieth you have stopped noticing whose they are, and by the fortieth your mother, standing behind your chair, has started saying the names to you under her breath before each man reaches the dais, so that you can say them first and watch his face.

Hervé de Penhoët comes near the end. He is a careful man of fifty in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword. He kneels, and puts his hands between yours, and says the words, all of them, clearly, and then three more.

"Saving the right of my house," he says, "in the duchy of Armance."

Behind you, your mother has gone completely still.

- Take his homage, saving and all, and say nothing.
- Tell him to swear plain, or not at all. [Even]
- Turn to your mother. It is her duchy.

**You choose:** Tell him to swear plain, or not at all.

"Swear plain, my lord," you say, "or swear another day." Your voice does not go up at the end. Hervé looks at you for a long moment, as a man looks at a field he has been told is his neighbour's. Then he says the words again, plain, without the saving, and rises, and bows lower than he did before.

*The household: more loyal; Mahaut's respect: higher*

### Penhoët's Claim

*Autumn 917, the third year of Queen Jehanne*

After Michaelmas Hervé de Penhoët puts it in writing, before the council, in his own neat hand.

The duchy of Armance, he says, descended by the male line for three hundred years, until the war gave it to a woman. His house is the old line's nearest male heir. Your mother holds it for her life; he does not dispute that; he is a loyal man. But at her death, he says, it should come to Penhoët by the custom of the Armance, and not to her daughter, who is Queen of the West already and has a crown to be going on with.

Then, at the bottom, under his seal, the offer. His son Ronan is twenty-one. If the Queen were to marry Ronan, the claim and the duchy would be in one bed, and there would be no quarrel to speak of, ever again.

"It's a good letter," says the chancellor, unhappily.

"It's an excellent letter," says your mother. "That is what I don't like about it."

- Hear the offer. Ask to meet Ronan de Penhoët.
- Answer that the Armance is your mother's, and then yours, and there is nothing to discuss.
- Answer that the Estates will settle the law, and the claim with it.

**You choose:** Hear the offer. Ask to meet Ronan de Penhoët.

Ronan de Penhoët comes to court at Martinmas: tall, quiet, his grandfather's shoulders and his father's patience, and none of either's habit of saying the clever thing. He bows to you, and to your mother, and talks to you for an hour about horses as if you were a person, which almost nobody at court does. You do not tell your mother that part.

*Penhoët: warmer*

### The Betrothal

*Winter 917, the third year of Queen Jehanne*

In the dark of the year the council talks about your marriage, as it has every winter since you were crowned, and this year they talk about it in front of you, because you are eleven, and a queen unpromised at eleven is a queen every court in the world is writing letters about.

Your father would not promise you when the Valdrennish came. The offers have kept coming since. Ronan de Penhoët, and the end of the Armance quarrel. Tanguy de Kerguen, the counterweight to Penhoët in the Armance, who is twenty-six and would like to be a king's consort more than he can quite hide. And no one at all, as your mother was no one's until she chose.

Your mother says, "Whatever you choose, choose it yourself." Your father, in the garden, says, "Choose the one who'll still be there when it goes wrong."

- Betroth yourself to Ronan de Penhoët.
- Betroth yourself to Tanguy de Kerguen.
- Promise yourself to no one. Not yet.

**You choose:** Betroth yourself to Tanguy de Kerguen.

Tanguy rides to Lannec in the snow to kneel to you, and does it with great style, and stays to dinner, and makes your father laugh twice. Kerguen and the crown, against Penhoët and the custom of the Armance. Hervé hears of it at dinner and finishes his dinner.

*Penhoët: much colder; Tanguy: much warmer*

Your father sits at the side of the dais in his chair with arms. Halfway through the feast he gets up, slowly, with a hand on your shoulder, and calls for silence, and the hall gives it to him at once, the way it never quite does for you.

"A toast," he says.

- Before he speaks, send to Hervé and seat him at the high table.

**You choose:** Stand, and make it with him.

You stand beside him, and he hears you get up, and gives you the cup instead of raising it. "To the West," you say, "which made my father a king and my mother a queen and me a bother." The hall laughs, and drinks, and your father sits down beaming, and tells everyone for a week that he was interrupted by his own daughter at his own feast.

*Plan's shadow: shorter; Standing in the realm: higher*

### The Marsh

*Summer 918, the fourth year of Queen Jehanne*

The lord of Quérec has been under guard at Lannec since the bridge, in a good room with a fire, waiting. The council has argued about him for two years. At midsummer your mother says the council will not decide it. You will. You are twelve.

You go to see him, once, before you decide. He gets up when you come in, and bows, which is not kneeling. "Well," he says. "You've grown."

- Pardon him, and give him the marsh, before the West.
- Send him over the march, and take his land for the crown.
- Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

**You choose:** Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

His son comes to court as your page, a boy of nine with his father's eyes, and is given a room near yours and a tutor and a pony, and every courtesy, and is never once let out of the walls of Lannec. Quérec swears to you that autumn. He has to.

*Standing in the realm: higher*

### Blanche

*Autumn 918, the fourth year of Queen Jehanne*

Your sister Blanche is eight, and the second most valuable girl in the West, and every great house has noticed. At Michaelmas the offers start coming for her too, as if she were a smaller crown.

Your mother says, "You'll have to decide what she is to you. Before someone else does."

- Keep her at court, at your side, where everyone can see.
- Send her to the Armance, to be your mother's.

**You choose:** Keep her at court, at your side, where everyone can see.

Blanche gets the room next to yours, and a place at the high table on your left, and sits through council with you, kicking the footstool. Every envoy who comes to court about her hand has to get past her first, and she has opinions about all of them.

*Blanche: much closer*

### The Regent

*Winter 918, the fourth year of Queen Jehanne*

In the winter you are twelve, and you have sat through three years of council, and you have stopped kicking the footstool, and you have started, without meaning to, to disagree with your mother in front of people.

She notices. Of course she notices. At Candlemas she sends for you to her chamber, with the old nurse, and the fire, and the list she keeps of every lord in the West and what he wants.

"The regency ends when you are sixteen," she says. "I would like to spend the years until then teaching you how to rule, and not how to quarrel with me. You would like," and she smiles, a little, "to rule now. I know. I was eighteen when my grandmother died and the King made me his ward, and I would have killed for a vote in my own council."

"Then give me one."

- Ask for a seat in council with a vote of your own. [Even]
- Say you will learn from her, and mean it.
- Go to your father, and ask him to speak for you.

**You choose:** Ask for a seat in council with a vote of your own.

"No," she says, gently. "Not yet. Ask me again in a year, and ask me better." It is the right answer. You know it is the right answer, which makes it worse.

*Plan's shadow: longer*

### The Queen's Guard

*Summer 919, the fifth year of Queen Jehanne*

At midsummer the captain of your guard dies, of a fever, at sixty: one of your father's old company, who carried you on his shoulders at your crowning so that you could see. The council has three names for his place.

His son, a solid, honest knight of thirty who has served in the guard since he was fifteen and has never once been surprised. An Armance knight of your mother's, Sire Guiomar, who held a gate at Mortefontaine with eleven men and is said never to have smiled since. And a young knight of your own choosing, twenty, a younger son with a good horse and no land, who has been at every tourney you have watched this year and has never once looked at the stands.

Davy Ludd says, "The son. He's dull. Dull's what you want at a door."

- The old captain's son. Your father's men keep your door.
- Sire Guiomar of the Armance. Your mother's men keep your door.
- The young knight. A queen should choose her own.

**You choose:** The young knight. A queen should choose her own.

He kneels to you on the tourney field in front of everyone, which was not the plan, and swears with a voice that cracks on "faith", and the whole court laughs, and he goes scarlet, and so do you. By Michaelmas the court has stopped laughing and started gossiping, and he still has not once looked at the stands.

*Plan's shadow: much shorter; The household: less loyal*

### The Salt Towns' Charter

*Autumn 919, the fifth year of Queen Jehanne*

At Michaelmas the burgesses of the salt towns come up the river to Lannec, six of them in good wool, with a charter already written out on the best parchment in the West, wanting only a seal.

It asks for the right to choose their own mayors, keep their own weights and scales, and pay the crown a fixed farm every Michaelmas instead of whatever the crown's men can find. Your father remitted their arrears, and they have not forgotten it. This is the next thing.

"Two hundred pounds a year," says the eldest of them, "in silver, for ever. Your Grace's father's men never got a hundred and fifty out of us in a good year."

The chancellor says the towns will be harder to tax in a bad year. Your mother says the towns will be harder to frighten in a bad year, which is not the same thing, and looks at you.

- Seal the charter.
- Seal it for three hundred, and see if they'll pay. [Even]
- Tell them a charter for ever is a matter for the Estates.

**You choose:** Seal the charter.

You seal it yourself, with the Great Seal, which takes both hands. The burgesses kneel, and pay the first year's farm on the spot, in silver, out of a chest they brought up the river on the chance. The chancellor counts it twice. It is all there.

### Before the Estates

*Spring 920, the sixth year of Queen Jehanne*

At Lady Day the summons goes out under the Great Seal, in your name, though you did not write it: the Estates of the West will sit at Lannec at Whitsun to settle the law of succession of the crown.

Nobody has ever written it down. The crown went to you because your father put it on your head. The Estates will decide whether that was a law, or a favour. You are fourteen. You are the case.

Hervé de Penhoët comes to court before the summons is a week old, himself, with his hat in his hands. "I'll vote for Your Grace's crown," he says. "The West has a queen, and I'm a loyal man. I ask only that Your Grace let the Estates write the custom of the Armance as it was: by the male line."

Your mother comes to your chamber that night. "He is offering you your crown," she says, "for my duchy. For your duchy, after me." She sits on the end of your bed, as she did when you were six. "I'll not tell you what to do. I'll tell you that I held it, and my grandmother held it, and I would like you to hold it after me."

In the garden, in the morning, your father says, "Well. You're the Queen."

You put the two answers side by side, and look at them, and do not give either. Not yet.

- Go on.

**You choose:** Go on.

### The End

Here Act I of Book One ends. You are Jehanne, Queen of the West, fourteen, under your mother's regency, with your father in the garden, betrothed to Tanguy de Kerguen, and the Estates summoned to decide whether you are a law or a favour.

Act II, The Law, is still being written.

# The Turncoat

Background: Household servant's son. Seed 3.

## The Destrier

*Spring, year 9 of King Aldred*

Ravell Hall, the stable yard, a raw spring morning, with mist lying in the moat and the jackdaws squabbling up and down the keep.

You sleep in the kennels with the lymers, curled against the warm one with the torn ear, and you smell like them, and do not care who knows it.

Your mother, Agnes, dresses Lady Ravell. You eat in the kitchen at the end of the long table nearest the draught, and you know more about the family than the family thinks. You know, for one thing, who the heir's real father is. You heard it through a privy wall two winters ago, in Lady Ravell's own voice, and you have told no one, not even your mother, and some nights it sits on your chest like a cat.

*(Continue)*

"Psst. You. Horse-boy."

Aymer Ravell is nine, a year older than you, the lord's second son: fair, quick, and bored out of his wits. He is leaning in the stable door with his hands behind his back, which means he wants something.

"I want to sit on Bayard," he says.

Bayard is his father's destrier, a grey stallion seventeen hands high who has killed one groom and crippled another. He is standing in the end stall with his ears pinned back, listening to every word.

"Just sit. That's all. Nobody'll know." Aymer gives you the smile that gets him anything he wants from anyone in the hall except his father. "Go on. You're not scared of him. Not you."

Master Ranulf, the marshal, is at the far end of the yard with his back to you, roaring at the farrier.

- Saddle Bayard for him and hold the head. *(Even)*
- Refuse him, and fetch Master Ranulf.
- Saddle him, and tell Aymer you will take the blame if you are caught.

**Chose:** Saddle him, and tell Aymer you will take the blame if you are caught.

You are caught, before Aymer is even off the horse. Master Ranulf beats you with a stirrup leather in front of the grooms, ten strokes, not hard by his standards.

Aymer watches from the gate and says nothing. That night he comes down to the kitchen, where he is never seen, with a honey cake from the high table wrapped in a napkin, and gives it to you in front of the scullions, and says he owes you. He means it. For now.

## Below Stairs

*Summer, year 9 of King Aldred*

Ravell Hall at night belongs to the servants.

When the family has gone up and the hall fire is banked, the kitchen comes alive: the cooks, the scullions, the grooms in from the yard, the laundry women, the men-at-arms off watch, all round the long table with the ends of the day's bread and the dregs of the day's ale. Old Hob Crane, the head groom, bent and profane, holds court at the top. Your mother sits at the far end, mending one of Lady Ravell's sleeves by rushlight, apart, because a tirewoman is not quite a servant like the others, and not quite anything else.

*(Continue)*

The laundry girls come in late with their arms red to the elbow from the copper. One of them, Nell, is your age, with a gap in her teeth and a laugh like a jay. She does the voices of the family for the kitchen: Lady Ravell's way of saying "Agnes," Lord Ravell's silence, Aymer's whine. The kitchen roars. Your mother does not look up from her sleeve.

Tonight Nell has a basket of wet sheets that should have been carried up to the drying-yard before dark, and was not, and she is looking round the table for somebody to help her carry it, and looking at you.

- Help Nell carry the sheets up to the drying-yard.
- Sit with Hob Crane and listen to him talk about horses.
- Go and sit by your mother, and help her with the sleeve.

**Chose:** Help Nell carry the sheets up to the drying-yard.

It is a heavy basket and a long stair, and the drying-yard is on the roof of the bakehouse, with the whole of the chase black around it and the stars out. You hang the sheets together in the dark, by feel. Nell does Master Ranulf's voice for you, and then your mother's, and then yours, which is not fair, because you do not sound like that.

When the sheets are up she sits on the parapet with her feet hanging over and says, "Do you ever think you'll get out of here?" You say you do not know. She says, "I will," and you believe her, for no reason, completely.

## The Wet Year

*Summer, year 11 of King Aldred*

Two good years, and then the wet one.

It starts raining at Whitsun, the summer you turn ten, and it does not stop. "It'll clear by Saint John's," the old men say, and then, "By Lammas, surely," and then they stop saying anything and just stand in their doorways watching it come down.

The barley lies flat in the fields and sprouts in the ear. The sheep cough in the folds. By Lammas bread costs three times what it did, and the bread is half bean flour and grit, and you can hear it crunch.

*(Continue)*

At Ravell Hall the high table still has three courses, and the scraps go out to the poor at the gate. Not all of them reach the gate. Nell from the laundry has a mother and five brothers in the village, and she has gone thin as a rake, and she still makes the kitchen laugh.

- Take a loaf from the high table scraps to Nell's mother before it reaches the gate.
- Set snares in Lord Ravell's chase after dark. *(Favorable)*
- Serve at the high table, where nobody is hungry, and keep your eyes down.
- Get into the tithe barn at night and fill a sack. *(Even)*

**Chose:** Set snares in Lord Ravell's chase after dark. (Favorable: success)

Two coneys the first week, a hare the second. You learn which paths the forester walks and when. Nobody at home asks where the meat comes from.

*Woodcraft +1*

## The Knight on the Road

At Michaelmas, when the worst of the wet year is over and nobody has yet believed it, a knight comes through.

He comes in at the gate of Ravell Hall, not as a guest, on the way to somewhere else with his horse lame in the off fore, and stops at the smithy, and the smith downs tools for him, because a knight does not wait.

*(Continue)*

He is not old, as knights go: about thirty, lean and brown, in a red surcoat badged with a black boar, the Earl of Carrow's. His harness is plain and well kept and has been mended in places. His horse is better than his harness. He has one squire, a sulky boy of sixteen in a better coat than his master's, who stands about kicking at the dogs.

He sees you watching from the smithy door. Everyone else has found a reason to be elsewhere; ordinary people do not stand about near knights. He looks at you with a kind of amusement, as if you were a puppy that had wandered into church, and says, "Well? Do you want to hold him, or don't you?"

- Hold his horse while the smith works.
- Ask him how a man becomes a knight.
- The squire kicks the smith's dog. Kick him back.
- Watch from a safe distance, and say nothing.

**Chose:** Hold his horse while the smith works.

You hold the great bay's head for an hour while the smith pares and fits, and the horse leans on you the whole time, and you do not move. The knight talks, not to you exactly, but near you: about the Earl's business in the north, about the price of a good horse, about the King, whom he has seen twice.

When the shoe is on he gives you a penny, which you expected, and asks your name, which you did not. "Pryce," he says, in return. "Walter Pryce. I was a younger son with a horse and a sword and nothing else. Now look at me." He looks down at his own mended harness and laughs. "Well. Look at me in ten years." Then he is gone up the road, and the squire never once looked at you.

## What a Boy Learns

*Summer, year 12 of King Aldred*

The wet year ends, as they all do. The next harvest comes in thin but enough, and people stop counting the beans in the sack, and the year after is ordinary, which is the best kind.

"You're getting tall," your mother says one day, sounding almost accusing, as if you had done it on purpose.

*(Continue)*

You are old enough now to be useful, and a boy of your sort has perhaps four years before he is a man and the shape of his life sets like mortar. There is time to get good at one thing, if someone will teach you.

Father Benet, the chaplain, teaches Aymer his letters and lets the servants' boys sit at the back if they keep quiet. Sir Bertram Oakes drills the household's young men at the pell every morning before Mass, and shouts "You're dead!" so often the grooms do it for a joke.

Whatever you choose, something else goes untaught.

- Learn your letters, Latin and Adalian, properly.
- Learn to fight with sword and buckler.
- Learn how gentlefolk eat, speak and stand, by serving at their tables.
- Learn horses and hounds, riding and the chase.

**Chose:** Learn how gentlefolk eat, speak and stand, by serving at their tables.

You learn to carve a capon in the proper joints, pour without spilling, kneel on the correct knee, and never speak first. It feels like learning to act a part. You suspect that is what it is.

*Courtesy +2*

## Midsummer Eve

*Spring, year 13 of King Aldred*

On Midsummer Eve the whole household that is not on duty goes down to Ravell village green, where the fire is built, and nobody is anybody's servant until morning.

There is a fiddler, and a man with a pipe and tabor, and a barrel that the lord pays for once a year, and a fire as tall as a house. The young men jump it, for luck and to be seen. The girls jump it in pairs, holding hands, for husbands. The old women watch who jumps with whom and remember.

*(Continue)*

You are twelve, and this year, for the first time, you are not one of the children running about in the dark beyond the firelight. You are standing at the edge of it, with the young men, trying to look as if you belong there.

Nell is in the middle of the dancing, doing Lady Ravell's walk, and half the household is weeping with laughter. She sees you. She does not look away.

- Ask Nell to jump the fire with you.
- Stay with the young men, and jump it alone.
- Walk Nell home the long way, when the fire burns down.
- Go home before the drinking starts.

**Chose:** Ask Nell to jump the fire with you.

She stops halfway through Lady Ravell's walk and takes your hand in front of everyone.

You run at the fire together, and it is much hotter close to than it looked, and for a moment you are in the middle of it with sparks going up all round you, and then you are through, on the other side, in the dark, still holding hands. Neither of you lets go for a while.

The old women saw. By Lammas the whole village has an opinion about it. Your mother's opinion is not expressed in words.

## Aymer Ravell

*Spring, year 14 of King Aldred*

By the next spring, you have stopped being a child in other people's eyes, and some of them do not like what they see instead.

*(Continue)*

Aymer Ravell is fourteen and has guests: two squires from Carrow's household, swaggering in good wool. To impress them, he told you to fetch his falcon, and then told them you'd stolen it. For the joke. They are laughing. One of them, a tall boy with good teeth, has his hand on his dagger, and he is not laughing at all.

- Tell the Carrow squire to his face that you are no thief, and stand there. *(Even)*
- Find the falcon where Aymer really hid it, and let the Carrow boys watch you do it. *(Even)*
- Laugh along with Aymer, take the blame, and talk to him alone later. *(Even)*
- Do nothing. Let it pass and keep your head down.

**Chose:** Find the falcon where Aymer really hid it, and let the Carrow boys watch you do it. (Even: success)

The falcon is in Aymer's own chest. The Carrow boys find that funny, at Aymer's cost. Aymer never quite forgives you for being clever in front of guests.

## Fourteen

*Autumn, year 15 of King Aldred*

It takes a year and a half for all that to settle, and in a village nothing settles completely. Then it is autumn again, and you are fourteen.

"Fourteen," your mother says, as if it were bad news. In a way it is. Fourteen is old enough to be bound apprentice, or put to the plough, or sent for a soldier. It is the age when a boy of your sort is either taken into somebody's service or left where he was born, for good.

*(Continue)*

Lord Ravell's household is choosing its pages and grooms for the year. Guests are coming for the autumn hunting, Sir Ancel Brome among them, and your mother has let out the seams of your best coat twice.

- Go and see what can be made of it.

**Chose:** Go and see what can be made of it.

## The Autumn Hunting

The Ravell household fills for the autumn hunting: knights, their wives, their hounds, their quarrels. Sir Ancel Brome comes down from the March with a lame horse and a bad temper. Sir Hamon Darrell comes from Ashby with neither horse nor money enough.

*(Continue)*

This is the year your mother has said you must be placed above the stables or not at all. Lady Ravell is watching you more closely than she used to. You wonder sometimes whether she guesses what you know.

Aymer is to go as squire to Sir Hamon in the spring. He has said, twice, that he wants you with him.

- Show Sir Ancel what the Ravell hounds and hawks can do in your hands. *(Favorable)*
- Ask Aymer to ask his father for you, as his fellow squire. *(Favorable)*
- Go with the hunt on the boar day, on foot, with a spear. *(Risky)*
- Go to Lady Ravell alone, and tell her what you heard through the privy wall. *(Even)*
- Keep your head down and hope to be noticed.

**Chose:** Go to Lady Ravell alone, and tell her what you heard through the privy wall. (Even: success)

She hears you out without moving. Then she asks what you want. You tell her. A week later Sir Ancel Brome is asked to take a boy from the household to the March, a long way from the hall. Lady Ravell never looks at you again if she can help it. Your mother does not know why.

## Leaving

*Spring, year 16 of King Aldred*

The winter after the choosing is your last one at home, though you do not know it until it is nearly over. Then in the spring the word comes, and all at once there are three days left, and then one.

*(Continue)*

You are not going far. That is the joke of it. Tomorrow you carry your bundle across the outer ward of Ravell Hall, from the stable loft where you have slept your whole life, to the knights' side of the house, as a knight's boy: not yet a squire, a long way from a knight. You will carry, clean, fetch, serve, and be hit, by better people than before.

*(Continue)*

Your mother straightens your collar four times. "Don't shame me," she says. She means: not in front of the lady. Nell is hanging sheets in the drying-yard, and has stopped hanging them.

- Go to Nell and tell her you will come back.
- Promise your mother you will never speak of the secret again, to anyone.
- Leave before dawn, before anyone can make you promise anything.

**Chose:** Leave before dawn, before anyone can make you promise anything.

The road is empty and the frost is hard. By the time the village wakes you are two miles gone. Nobody will say you broke a promise. Nobody will say you made one, either.

## Ravell Hall

You have lived at Ravell Hall all your life without ever once coming in by the front.

You know every stone of it from the back. Which drain floods. Which door of the buttery sticks. Where the kitchen cats have their kittens. Which of the grooms will cover for you and which will not. You know the castle the way a mouse knows a house.

Now you cross the outer ward with your bundle on your shoulder, past the stables where you slept last night, and nobody stops you, and that is the strange part. Hob Crane, the head groom, sees you go by and raises a hand, then lowers it, as if he is not sure he is still allowed to. A laundress you have known since you could walk looks at you twice, the second time as if you were somebody else.

The house has not changed. You are on the other side of it now, and from this side it looks different. It is bigger, and colder, and full of people you have served at table who have never once looked at your face.

*(Continue)*

Lord Thurstan Ravell holds this castle and eleven manors from the King. He holds four knights' fees under him: four knights who owe him forty days' service a year, mounted and armed, in return for their land. Sir Hamon Darrell of Ashby and Sir Ancel Brome of Brome Tower are two of those four. Their households live under his roof for half the year, eat his bread, and fight his quarrels. Yours will too.

Sir Ancel meets you in the yard without getting down from his horse. He is lean as a hayrake, burned dark by the March wind, with half his left ear gone to a Caldmoor sword at Harrow Ford and grey coming into his beard. He looks you up and down like a horse he has been sold. "You're late," he says. You are not late; you came the day you were told. You do not say so. Something in his face eases, slightly, at your not saying so. "Stables," he says. "Then find Cobb. Then find me."

*(Continue)*

You will sleep in the squires' loft over the great chamber, with four others, on straw pallets under a roof that drips in one corner. The loft has its own rules, and nobody tells them to you; you learn them by breaking them.

The eldest of the four is Giles Marrick, nineteen, the second son of a Carrow knight, with good teeth and a better sword and a way of smiling at the side of your head instead of your face. He looks at your boots, which are your father's, then your hands, which are not a gentleman's, then your face, and decides something. You watch him decide it.

Aymer Ravell is there too: the lord's younger son, sixteen, fair, quick, restless, already bored with you and everyone else. He knew you when you were both small enough to hide in the hay. He looks at you now the way a man looks at an old coat he forgot he owned. "Well, look who's come up in the world," he says, and grins, and it is not quite friendly and not quite not.

- Kneel to your master in the yard, properly, as if you had been doing it all your life. *(Even)*
- Go straight to the stables and make yourself useful before anyone tells you to.
- Find someone in the hall who might tell you how things are done here.

**Chose:** Find someone in the hall who might tell you how things are done here.

Everybody is too busy. You try the steward's clerk, who tells you to ask the steward. You try the steward, who looks through you. You try a cook, who throws a turnip at you, not hard.

Then you try the young woman in the good grey gown who is crossing the inner ward with a basket of thread, because she is the only person in the castle walking slowly.

She stops. She has a narrow, clever face, dark hair under a linen cap, ink on her right forefinger, and an expression that suggests she has been waiting all day for something interesting to happen and is prepared to accept you as a poor substitute.

"You're new," she says. "You have the look. Everyone has it the first week. Like a calf at a fair." She tells you, in about the time it takes to say a paternoster, where to wash, where to eat, which of the stewards to avoid (the fat one), which of the cooks will give you bread if you ask nicely (the one who threw the turnip), how to tell the lord's chamber from the lady's in the dark, and that Lady Ravell can hear through walls and likely through floors.

"I am Isabel Sayer," she says. "I wait on Lady Ravell. My father is a knight with a hundred acres in Hollesby and seven daughters, and I am the fourth, so I am as poor as you are, only better dressed. Don't tell anyone I talk to grooms."

You are not a groom. You do not say so. She sees you not say so, and smiles for the first time, and goes off with her basket. You stand in the inner ward for some time after.

## The First Night

Sir Ancel's household sleeps in the hall of Brome Tower, round the hearth, on pallets, the knight himself in the chamber above with the door open and his sword on the bed.

You have a place by the wall, which is the worst place, because it is the coldest and furthest from the light. Nobody tells you so. Nobody tells you anything. You lie in the dark with your bundle under your head and listen to the sounds of a house that is not yours.

*(Continue)*

Somebody stops by your place in the dark on the way to the privy. It is Will Cobb, the serjeant of archers, in his shirt, with his breath smoking. "New," he says. It is not a question. "You'll be cold tonight. Tomorrow you'll be too tired to notice."

- Get up and walk with him, and ask him about this place.
- Lie awake, and think about home.
- Get up before anyone, and have your work done before they wake.

**Chose:** Get up and walk with him, and ask him about this place.

Cobb walks you round the outside of the tower in the dark, slowly, with his hands in his armpits, and tells you everything you need to know and nothing you do not. Which of Sir Ancel's men will steal from you. Which will stand by you. Where the wall is low enough to see Thorne's lights down the valley. "Sir Ancel says nothing," he says. "When he's pleased, he says 'Hm.' When he's not, you'll know." At the barn door he says, "Get some sleep," and then, "You'll do," which you think about for a week.

## The High Table

*Summer, year 16 of King Aldred*

By Midsummer you have learned the first lesson of service, which is that nobody will tell you anything twice, and the second, which is that nobody will tell you anything once.

*(Continue)*

It must be carved in its proper joints, laid on trenchers in the proper order, and presented with the proper words, while forty people watch and the Earl of Carrow's man judges Lord Ravell's household by how it is done. Giles Marrick has done it before. He has offered to help you, which means he will not.

## Giles Marrick

## Sharp Steel

"Wasters," says Sir Bertram Oakes, with contempt, kicking a pile of wooden practice swords. "You lot have learned to fight with sticks, and you'll die like it."

Today it is sharp swords, at half speed, in the tilt-yard, in front of Lady Ravell's women, who have come down with cushions to watch. A real edge on a real blade, catching the sun. The squires look at each other. Nobody laughs.

"You," says Sir Bertram, and points his two-fingered hand at you. "First."

- Take the sword. *(Even)*
- Let one of the gentlemen's sons go first.

**Chose:** Take the sword. (Even: success)

Half speed with a sharp blade is slower than you would think, and more frightening. You touch Sir Bertram's arm once and he touches you nowhere. He nods. That is a great deal, from him.

## Winter at the Pell

*Winter, year 16 of King Aldred*

Snow on the moat, ice in the horse-trough, and the hall so full of smoke from Martinmas to Candlemas that everyone at Ravell coughs like Master Gervase.

"Winter," Sir Bertram says, blowing on his two-fingered hand, "is when boys turn into something or don't."

*(Continue)*

It is the first winter, and you are learning that a household in winter is a school with no master and forty teachers. Sir Bertram takes the young men to the pell every morning before Mass, whatever the weather, and shouts "You're dead!" until the rooks leave. Master Ranulf breaks the new horses in the covered ride. Father Benet keeps the lord's accounts in a cold room over the chapel, and will let a boy sit in if he holds his tongue and brings his own candle. Lady Ravell keeps her own hall in the evenings, with music, where the young men learn to be tolerable company or are not asked back.

You have the winter. You cannot be everywhere.

- Sir Bertram's pell, every morning, until your arms stop shaking.
- The covered ride with Master Ranulf and the young horses.
- Father Benet's cold room, and the lord's accounts.
- Lady Ravell's hall, where the young men learn to be company.

**Chose:** The covered ride with Master Ranulf and the young horses.

"Again," says Master Ranulf, without looking up from the young horse he is soothing. "And this time stay on."

You are thrown nine times before Candlemas, once into the water-trough, through the ice. The grooms keep count on the stable door in chalk. By Lady Day the count stops, because you can sit a horse that does not want you and turn it with your knees while your hands do something else. Ranulf rubs the count off the door with his sleeve and says nothing. You know what it means.

## Sir Ancel's Cattle

*Summer, year 17 of King Aldred*

Brome Tower is three days north of Ravell, on the March, where the King's peace is a rumour and every man sleeps with a knife.

In June forty of Sir Ancel's cattle are driven off in the night, and his herdsman, an old man called Alun who used to give you cheese, is found face down in the burn with his head stove in.

Sir Ancel says nothing at all for an hour. Then he says, to the fire, that he will ride to Thorne's mill tonight and burn it with Thorne's miller inside.

Lord Ravell has written that there will be no private war on the March this year, by the King's order. Sir Ancel reads the letter twice and uses it to light the fire.

- ~~Offer to write out Sir Ancel's plaint to Lord Ravell's court instead.~~ *(Risky, Requires: can read)*

## Errand to Wendham

"Wax, pepper, a bolt of russet, two hundred horseshoe nails, and this," your master says, handing you a sealed letter, "for Master Osbert Fuller at the guild hall. About a debt. Don't read it. Don't lose it. Don't come back drunk."

Wendham on market day is a din of carts and hawkers and pigs loose in the street, and girls with baskets, and Sarenzan sailors with gold in their ears, and the smell of the tan-pits over everything. It will take all day if you are quick, and two if you are not, and there is a great deal in Wendham to make a young man not quick.

- Deliver the letter to Master Fuller in person, and stay to dinner if asked.

## Christmas at Sauvemer

*Autumn, year 23 of King Aldred*

The Queen comes over from Adalia with her household for Christmas, and there is a feast in the great hall of Sauvemer castle, which a year ago belonged to a man who died of the flux.

It is the strangest night of your life. Six months ago you were on the dyke at Les Salines with the water rising. Tonight you are in a hall hung with Valdrennish banners turned upside down, eating swan, with the Queen's ladies at the high table in silk the colour of the sea.

*(Continue)*

One of the ladies is Lady Alys Fane, the Earl of Westry's daughter. She is nineteen, tall, fair, and bored to death by a young lord who has been telling her about his horse for an hour. When the dancing starts, she looks down the hall for someone, anyone, who is not him.

Héloïse de Corbie is at the feast too, at the far end of a lower table with the other Valdrennish women of rank who are the King's guests until their kinsmen's ransoms are paid. She is watching the Adalians celebrate. She is not smiling.

**Chose:** Ask Lady Alys to dance, before the young lord can. (Favorable: success)

She looks at your hands, which are not a gentleman's, and at your face, and says yes before the young lord has finished getting up. At the end she asks what you did at Les Salines, and listens to the answer, and asks about Grisolles, which nobody at court ever does.

## The Truce of Saint-Lys

*Winter, year 23 of King Aldred*

King Aldred would fight on, if he could pay for it. He cannot. Nor can King Amaury, who has lost an army on the salt pans and needs ten years and a great deal of borrowed money to raise another.

In Lent, the Lanzi house tells King Aldred that it will lend him nothing more until he treats. In the same week, the Varesco house tells King Amaury the same thing, in almost the same words. The Signory of Sarenza has lent too much to both to let either one fight to the end.

*(Continue)*

The Pontiff's legate, Cardinal Orsino Brancale, a soft-spoken Sarenzan with a merchant's eyes, rides between the two courts with the bankers' letters in his saddlebag. At Saint-Lys, at Lady Day, he gets his truce: three years, and the terms are the terms of the side that won at Les Salines.

Sauvemer and all its march are Adalian, held of no one. The Duke of Armance comes home, exchanged for Thibaut de Brésy and the duke taken on the salt, and the Armance is to be left in peace. The prisoners of Grisolles are released at rates the bankers fix, which nobody likes. And the wool goes back to Vervais, as the Moot was promised.

It is not the crown of Valdrenne. Nobody in the army ever really thought it would be, after the Lisonne. It is a port, a march, an ally kept, and a King of Valdrenne who has learned what it costs to fight one. Men argue in the taverns of Sauvemer for months about whether that was worth Grisolles. Most decide it was. The ones who were in the rearguard are not so sure.

*(Continue)*

Half the army goes home. The rest stays, to garrison Sauvemer and its march, and to be paid even more slowly than before.

- Stay with the garrison in Sauvemer. That is where the King's grants will be given, when they are given.

**Chose:** Stay with the garrison in Sauvemer. That is where the King's grants will be given, when they are given.

You take a house in the Rue des Tanneurs with your men: two rooms, a yard, a well, and neighbours who will not look you in the eye. Sauvemer is the King's now, and in a sense yours. It does not feel like it.

## The Flux

The flux comes through the camp again in the heat. It always does.

You hear it before you see it: men groaning in the night, men running for the ditches, men who do not get there. Men who came through Grisolles without a scratch die of it in a week, squatting in the ditches, too weak to stand, and are buried in the ditches by men who will be in them next.

"Don't drink the water," the old soldiers say. "Drink the wine." There is no wine.

- Make your men boil everything they drink, and dig their latrines away from the well.
- There is nothing to be done. Trust to luck. *(Even)*

**Chose:** Make your men boil everything they drink, and dig their latrines away from the well.

They think you are mad, and do it anyway, because you are watching. Your company loses nobody to the flux that summer. The company next door loses nine.

## The Garrison

*Spring, year 24 of King Aldred*

A truce is a strange thing to live in. You are a conqueror in a conquered country, which sounds grand. In fact it is boredom, cold, short pay, and the feeling of being watched.

The Sauvemer people have not forgiven the siege and will not, in your lifetime. The Adalian garrison drinks, gambles, marries local girls who would rather not, quarrels with the townsmen, and sometimes kills one. The townsmen sometimes kill one back. Every month there is a hanging.

*(Continue)*

Your following has changed since Saltcombe. It is smaller: the war and the flux have seen to that. Davy Ludd is seventeen and taller than you.

"Boring," says Davy, sitting on the well-head, kicking his heels. "I never thought I'd say it. I'd give a month's pay for a Valdrennish army to come over the hill." "You haven't had a month's pay since Lent," says somebody. "That's why I can afford to give it."

You have time, for the first time in years, to choose what to do with it.

- Train your men properly, every day, as if the war will start again tomorrow.
- Learn the town. The language, the people, who owes whom.
- Spend your evenings where the women of rank are.
- Put your money to work. Cloth, wine, ransoms: there is money to be made in a garrison town by a man who can count. *(Even)*

**Chose:** Train your men properly, every day, as if the war will start again tomorrow.

It will start again. Everyone knows the truce will end. When it does, your men will be the best-drilled company in Sauvemer, and they will know it, and so will the men who give out grants.

## The Iron Company

*Autumn, year 24 of King Aldred*

In the first winter of the truce the Iron Company comes west: eight hundred Hroswald mercenaries under Ulric Rotbart, who rode behind the army all the way back from Grisolles and stood on the heights at Les Salines, and whom King Amaury has stopped paying now that he has no war to pay them for.

A free company without a war is a plague with a banner. It takes a village a week and a town a month. The Valdrennish crown will not fight it, because it might be useful later. The Adalian garrison will not fight it, because there is a truce.

*(Continue)*

It comes into the march country behind Sauvemer, and the villages there, which were stripped once by the siege, are stripped again by them. A delegation of their headmen comes to the garrison to ask the Adalians for protection, since the Adalians are now their lords. The garrison captain laughs at them.

Their spokesman is an old salt-boiler with burned forearms and no front teeth. "You took our town," he says, through a Valdrennish priest who translates as if every word hurt him. "So you're our lords now. Fine. Then lord."

They come to you next.

## A Ship for Saltcombe

*Winter, year 24 of King Aldred*

In the spring of the second year of the truce, a ship sails from Sauvemer for Saltcombe with the garrison's letters, and a few places for men on leave.

You have been away for four years. You have letters from home that are a year old. The passage costs a pound. The journey home and back will take the whole summer.

The King's grants for the conquered lands are to be decided at Michaelmas, in Sauvemer. A man who is away when grants are decided is easy to forget.

- Stay. Grants are given to men who are there.

**Chose:** Go home, for the summer.

You are on the ship with the letters three days later. The Narrow Sea is calm this time. You stand at the rail all the way, watching for the white cliffs that are not white at all, close to, but grey and streaked with green.

## Home

Everything at home is smaller than you remember it.

Ravell Hall is the same, and the faces are not. Hob Crane is dead. Master Ranulf is half blind. Your mother is still Lady Ravell's tirewoman, and still straightens your collar. Nell is in the dairy now, and has a child that is not yours, and is not married.

*(Continue)*

The March is quiet now, the way a burned field is quiet. Everyone at home talks about Harlow Moss, where the King broke the Caldmoor host in the snow. In the March they talk about the winter before it, when the towers held and everything outside them burned.

**Chose:** Spend the summer with your family.

It is a quiet summer, and the best one you have had in years. At the end of it your family stand in the lane and watch you go, and this time you look back.

## Ravell, in Passing

On the way back to Saltcombe you stop at Ravell, because everyone stops at Ravell.

Lord Ravell came home at Lady Day, after nearly three years in a Valdrennish castle. The ransom has stripped the household to the walls: the plate, the best horses, two manors mortgaged to the Lanzi, half the hangings in the hall. He is thinner and greyer and does not ride out any more. Lady Ravell runs everything now, as she always did, but openly.

Sir Hamon is there, home from the war, living on what is left of Ashby. He feeds you until you cannot move and asks about Les Salines four times, and each time listens as if it were the first.

Isabel Sayer is still with Lady Ravell. She sees you across the inner ward and stops walking.

- Go on to Saltcombe.

**Chose:** Find Isabel Sayer.

## The King's Grants

*Summer, year 25 of King Aldred*

At Michaelmas the King's council sits in the great hall of Sauvemer castle to give out the conquered lands.

It is not a generous business. The King wants loyal men holding the country, men who will defend it because it is theirs, and he wants it cheap. So the great lords get towns and castles, and the bannerets get manors, and the rest get what is left, and the duty to hold it. "Men get paid where they bled," the old soldiers say. ## News from the South

*Autumn, year 25 of King Aldred*

That winter the Sarenzan galleys come into Sauvemer harbour late, and fewer of them, and the sailors bring a story.

In the east, they say, beyond the Midsea, there is a sickness that came off a caravan. Now it is in the Midsea ports. It begins with a cough and a fever, and then the skin goes mottled, grey and dark like a bruise that spreads, and then the cough brings up blood. Three days. Sometimes one. The sailors call it the Mottle. In Vellagio, they say, the Signory has shut the gates and nobody goes in or out, and the bells have stopped because there is nobody left to ring them.

*(Continue)*

"Fairy tales," says the harbour-master, laughing. "Last year it was a sea-serpent off Vellagio. Year before, a mermaid." The sailor who told it does not laugh. He has stopped drinking, and keeps looking at his own hands.

Nobody believes it, quite. Sailors' stories always grow.

In the spring you will go to Ormel.

## The Bell

The dead woman is Rousse the netmaker, who was a widow, and she died this morning, after three days of coughing, with her skin gone the grey-dark of a bad bruise from her throat to her belly.

## Behind the Walls

Sauvemer has shut its gates against the country too, but it opens them for a lord with a writ, and you take rooms over a tavern by the harbour and wait.

## The Reckoning of the Dead

*Winter, year 26 of King Aldred*

The frost comes at Martinmas, and with the frost, the Mottle goes, the way a tide goes out. Nobody can say why. The coughing stops. The bells stop. People come out of their houses and look at each other in the street as if they had not seen each other for years, which in a way they have not.

*(Continue)*

Bastien comes to the church at the end and leans in the doorway. "Lost my brother," he says. "Lost my brother's wife. Lost the eel-traps, nobody to set 'em." He spits. "Still here." He looks at you. "So are you. I'll give you that."

In the spring the swallows come back, as if nothing had happened.

The people who are left look at you differently from the way they looked at you on the day of the bell. They look away when you pass, and talk when you have gone.

[break]

You are a lord of a manor that has lived through the end of the world. Now you have to find out how to live in what comes after.

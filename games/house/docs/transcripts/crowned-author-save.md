### The King at Lannec

*Spring 912, the second year of King David*

The great hall at Lannec is cold in the mornings, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the dais yet, and the chamberlain stands at your elbow with the day's petitions under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

You were an archer's son once. Now the hall stands when you come in, and the hall is the hall where the Estates of the West made you king, and every morning you walk past the place where you stood that day and do not look at it. You told them, in the first year of your reign, that when you were gone they would choose again, as they chose you. You meant it at the time.

Mahaut comes down with her women. She is thirty-one, and Queen of the West, and Duchess of Armance in her own right, which she has never once let a clerk forget. She takes the chair beside yours and moves the salt so you can reach it. Jehan comes after her at a run, five years old, in a tunic he has already torn, and climbs into your lap without asking, as he has since he could climb. The younger ones come behind with their nurse, in order of size.

"Your son," says Mahaut, "has been in the stables again."

"My son," you say, "is going to be King of the West. He can be in the stables if he likes."

"If the Estates say so," says Mahaut. "Until they do, the stables are where the grooms will tell everyone he was."

- Take Jehan up on your saddle and ride out to the fields.
- Go through the treasurer's rolls before the petitioners come.
- Hear the petitions in the great hall yourself.

**You choose:** Take Jehan up on your saddle and ride out to the fields.

You ride out by the river gate with Jehan in front of you on the saddle, holding the reins he thinks are steering the horse. The people on the road stop and bow and stare. "Why are they looking?" he asks. "Because you're going to be their king." He thinks about that for a furlong. "Can I have a horse of my own, then?"

### The Old Company

*Spring 912, the second year of King David*

Davy Ludd comes up from the guardroom after Terce, the way he has every day since there was a guardroom to come up from. He is grey now, and walks with one shoulder lower than the other, and he does not bow to the King of the West unless there is somebody watching.

"There's fewer of us," he says, and sits without asking, and pours himself your wine. "At the Christmas feast. You've noticed."

### The Heir of the West

*Summer 912, the second year of King David*

Midsummer, and the court at Lannec in the long gallery out of the heat. You watch Jehan across the gallery, trying to make the Queen's greyhound jump over a stool, and getting the stool over the greyhound instead.

There has only been one crown of the West, and you are wearing it, and the Estates gave it to you. In the first year of your reign you told them that when you were gone they would give it again, to whom they chose. You had no child in the hall that day. You have one now.

"They will choose someone else," Mahaut says, beside you, low. "Not now. Now you are alive. When you're old, and he's a child, and there's a man somewhere with a better claim and a grown beard." She watches her son. "My grandmother held the Armance through a war, a woman alone, and nobody chose her. She held it anyway. He'll need to know how."

"Then who teaches him?"

Mahaut takes him in hand that summer: the council chamber, the household accounts, the order of precedence at a feast, which takes a month. By Michaelmas Jehan can tell a Kerguen from a Quérec across a hall by the way they stand, and says so, loudly, which Mahaut is working on.

### Quérec

*Summer 912, the second year of King David*

The summer court ends with the oaths, as it does every year: the lords of the West renew their faith to the King, and this year, for the first time, you have asked them to swear to Jehan as your heir.

They swear, one after another, and kiss the child's small hand, and he bears it with a face like a bishop. Then Bertrand de Quérec comes forward, and stands, and does not kneel.

He is the son of the man you sent over the march in a cart. Quérec is the crown's now. He has come back under the peace and a safe-conduct, landless, with his father's eyes, to say what his father would have said.

"You were chosen," Bertrand says, loud enough for the hall. "The Estates of the West chose you, in this hall, and you told them they'd choose again. I heard you. So did they." ### The Embassy

*Winter 912, the second year of King David*

At Martinmas an embassy comes over the hills from Cordelle with forty horses and a bishop, to ask for the heir of the West.

King Amaury VII offers his daughter, Princess Isabeau, six, for Jehan. A betrothal now, a marriage when the children are of age, and peace on the march for as long as it lasts. The bishop puts it beautifully. The Valdrennish lords stand behind him and look at the hall as if pricing it.

Mahaut listens to all of it without moving. When they have gone to their lodgings she says, "Amaury tried to marry me to his brother when the boy was eleven. Now he wants my son for his sister. They are a persistent family." She turns her rings. "My grandmother let me choose. I chose you."

You bought the peace with Valdrenne in coin. Amaury is offering to make it a family, which is cheaper and lasts longer, or does not.

**You choose:** Send them home. He will choose, as his mother did.

You send them home with gifts and a refusal so courteous that it takes the bishop a day's ride to understand it. Mahaut, at supper, puts her hand over yours on the table, and leaves it there.

### The Estates at Whitsun

*Spring 914, the fourth year of King David*

You call the Estates of the West to Lannec at Whitsun in 914, and they come: the lords on their benches, the bishops in their stalls, the burgesses of the salt towns at the back in their good wool, all of them in the hall where they made you king.

You have called them for one thing. You told them they would choose your successor. Bertrand de Quérec is in the hall, and nods to you, and nods to Jehan. Hervé de Penhoët sits on the Armance bench among the Queen's own lords, saying nothing, and taking notes.

### The Illness

*Winter 914, the fourth year of King David*

It comes at Christmas, with the great hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there. Mahaut is sitting beside it in her furs with a book of accounts on her knee, and turns a page, and looks up when you move, and you see on her face what you look like. Jehan is asleep at the foot of the bed in a nest of cloaks, with his shoes on.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse.

"Well," says Davy, from the door. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"There is a Sarenzan physician in the town," says Mahaut, "who cured a bishop. There is the relic of Saint Gildas at the abbey. And there is lying still and doing what you're told." She closes the book. "I would choose the third. You won't."

- Send for the Sarenzan. (£10) [Even]
- Have the saint's finger brought from the abbey. [Even]
- Lie still and do as Mahaut says, for once.

**You choose:** Lie still and do as Mahaut says, for once.

You lie still. Mahaut runs the kingdom from a chair beside your bed for the whole of Lent, and runs it well, and tells you about it every evening whether you want to hear or not. It is the first winter in thirty years you have not ridden out. The West, you notice, does not fall down.

### The Crowning

*Spring 915, the fifth year of King David*

Lady Day, the new year. You sit in the gallery in a chair with a fur over your knees, and you know.

You will live. The physicians say so, and you believe them, and you can feel it: you will sit in the sun for years yet. But you will not ride to the march again, or sit a whole day's assize, or hold a great council by the force of standing up in it. The kingdom cannot wait for you to get better. It can only wait for Jehan to grow up.

The kings of Valdrenne used to do it this way, in the old days, when they were afraid: crown the heir while the father lived, so that there was never a day without a crowned head. Jehan is eight. His brothers and sisters are younger. Mahaut would govern for him. You would be there, behind the throne, for as long as you last.

You promised the Estates they would choose. They have not chosen. To crown him now is to choose for them, and every man in the hall will know it. Bishop Évrard of Saint-Lys, who crowned you, is a hundred and one, and has sent word that he would like to crown one more, if it is soon.

- Crown him at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.
- Crown him quietly in the palace chapel, and tell the West after.
- Crown him, and name his council in the same hour, so no one else can.

**You choose:** Crown him at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.

They carry you into the cathedral in a chair, and you stand for the crowning, because you will not sit for it. Old Évrard sets a small crown on Jehan's head with hands that shake so badly two canons have to steady them, and the oldest man in the West crowns the youngest, and the nave roars. Jehan does not cry, and does not smile, and does not drop the sceptre, which is heavier than he is. Afterwards, in the sacristy, he is sick into a basin, and then asks for his dinner.

*Standing in the realm: much higher; The household: more loyal*

## The Heir

> Woe to the land whose king is a child, said the preacher. He had not met this one.

### The Council

*Summer 915, the first year of King Jehan*

You are eight years old, and King of the West, and the chair in the council chamber was made for your father. Your feet do not reach the floor. Somebody has put a footstool under them, tactfully, and you would like to kick it across the room.

Your mother sits at the other end of the table, and governs. She is the regent. That is a word you have learned this spring, along with "minority", "the Great Seal", and "Your Grace", which is what the chancellor calls you now, every time, as if checking it still works.

Your father is in his rooms along the gallery. He is better. He sits in the sun in the mornings, and you go and see him after council, and he asks you what they said, and laughs.

Today the council is talking about Quérec. He swore to you, and kissed your hand, and has sent a letter congratulating your mother on her regency. The council thinks that is worse. They talk across you, the way grown men talk across a child at dinner. Nobody asks you anything.

- Ask a question, out loud, in the middle of it. [Favorable]
- Say nothing, and count who agrees with whom.
- Go to your father after, and ask him what to say.

**You choose:** Ask a question, out loud, in the middle of it.

"What does Quérec want?" you say. It comes out loud. The council stops. The chancellor opens his mouth and shuts it. Your mother looks at you for a long moment, and then says, "That is the question, my lords. Answer the King." They try. None of them knows. You see your mother see that.

### The Regency

*Autumn 915, the first year of King Jehan*

After Michaelmas your mother sends for you to her own chamber, with only her old nurse present, the way she once sent for your father when they were deciding something nobody could know yet.

"The council," she says, "is your father's men, and my men, and they hate each other quietly, which is the best kind of council. I want to change it." She puts a list in front of you. Half the names on it are Armance: her people, her cousins, her grandmother's old captains. "These men held the Armance for a woman through a war. Your father's men put a crown on a man. I think you need the first kind more."

"Why are you asking me?" you say.

"Because you are the King," she says, "and one day you will remember who I asked."

- Give her the Armance council she wants.
- Keep your father's men. They made the crown.
- Take half her names, and keep half his. [Favorable]

**You choose:** Take half her names, and keep half his.

You go through the list with her, name by name, and keep the ones who have been to the march and the marsh, and lose the ones who only talk. She argues. You argue back. At the end she sits back and looks at you and says, "Your father would have kept all his," and you cannot tell whether she is pleased.

### The Marsh

*Winter 915, the first year of King Jehan*

At Candlemas the oath Bertrand swore in the great hall, with your father's guard along the wall, turns out to have been worth what such oaths are worth. He takes the crown's salt-works at the edge of the marsh, and the toll bridge, and says in a letter to every lord in the West that he swore to a King, not to a child and his mother.

His father's old tenants have risen for him, and the marsh lords who remember his father. Forty marsh lords are with him, and two hundred men, and a ship from Valdrenne has been seen off the salt flats, though nobody can say whose.

The council meets for three days. The constable wants to march at once, in the frost. The chancellor wants to treat. Your mother listens to both and says nothing, which you have learned means she has decided and is waiting to see if anyone agrees.

On the third day she turns to you. "It is your crown he has hung on that bridge," she says. "What do you want?"

- March, and ride with the host yourself, under your own banner. [Even]
- Treat with him. Offer a pardon for the bridge.
- Wait for spring, and let him freeze in his marsh.

**You choose:** March, and ride with the host yourself, under your own banner.

They put you on a grey pony in a cloak of the royal colours, in the middle of six hundred men, and the constable does the rest. At the toll bridge the marsh men see the child king's banner coming over the causeway in the frost and lose their nerve, half of them, before anyone has drawn. Bertrand holds the tower for two days, and then asks for terms. Your mother gives him the terms, in your name, and makes you watch her do it.

### The King's Question

*Spring 916, the second year of King Jehan*

In the spring your father is well enough to sit in the garden at the foot of the palace wall, in the sun, with a blanket and a cup of wine he is not supposed to have.

You sit with him most mornings, after council. Today he talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from: an archer's son, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About your mother, the garden at Lannec, the hawk on her fist that she did not need. About the war, and the bridge, and the Estates, and the morning they made him king and he stood in the hall and could not feel his own feet.

Then he stops, and looks at you, and you can see him decide to say it.

"You're nine," he says. "You've had a year of it. Bridges and councils and your mother." He takes your hand. His is dry and light. "Tell me. Are you going to be me?"

- "I'll try to be."
- "No. I'm going to be me."
- Say nothing, and hold his hand.

**You choose:** "No. I'm going to be me."

He laughs, which hurts him, and grips your hand. "Good," he says. "Good. One of me was plenty."

### The Bishop of Saint-Lys

*Summer 916, the second year of King Jehan*

Bishop Évrard of Saint-Lys dies at midsummer, at a hundred and two, in the middle of the Office, between one psalm and the next. He crowned your father. He crowned you. The whole West comes to bury him, and you stand at the front of the nave in black with your mother, and try not to look at the place where he set the crown on your head.

Then the chapter of Saint-Lys must choose a new bishop, and the crown has a voice in the choosing, and so, everyone knows, does Cordelle, and so does Wendmere.

There are three names. Your mother's confessor, a quiet Armance canon who has never once disagreed with her. Your father's old chancellor-clerk, Master Anselm, who wrote the patent of the crown and knows where everything is buried. **You choose:** Speak for Master Anselm, who knows where everything is.

Master Anselm becomes Bishop of Saint-Lys, the first born in the West, and comes to see your father in the garden the day after, and they laugh together for an hour about things neither will tell you. He will bless what the crown wants. He will also remember what the crown owes him.

### The Acclamation

*Spring 917, the third year of King Jehan*

Lady Day, 917. You are ten. The Estates of the West meet at Lannec to acclaim you, in person, as their King: not as a child with a crown on, but as the King they will obey. They did not choose you. Your father chose for them. Today they will say whether they agree.

The hall is full. Your father sits at the side of the dais in a chair with arms, where everyone can see him and nobody can say he is ruling. Your mother stands behind your chair, which is the old King's chair, with the footstool gone, because you have grown. Bertrand is on the marsh lords' bench, under guard, and will kneel today whether he likes it or not.

The chancellor has written you a speech. It is good, and long, and in Latin at the end. Your mother has read it and said nothing, which means she does not like it. Your father has read it and said, "Say what you like. They'll remember that."

- Put the speech down, and say something of your own. [Favorable]

## Book One: The Keeper

### The Homage of the Lords

*Summer 917, the third year of King Jehan*

At midsummer the great lords of the West come to Lannec to do you homage in person, now that the Estates have acclaimed you: every man who holds of the crown, one by one, on his knees, with his hands between yours.

Your hands are small. The lords' hands are not. By the twentieth you have stopped noticing whose they are, and by the fortieth your mother, standing behind your chair, has started saying the names to you under her breath before each man reaches the dais, so that you can say them first and watch his face.

Hervé de Penhoët comes near the end. He is a careful man of fifty in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword. He kneels, and puts his hands between yours, and says the words, all of them, clearly, and then three more.

"Saving the right of my house," he says, "in the duchy of Armance."

Behind you, your mother has gone completely still.

- Take his homage, saving and all, and say nothing.
- Tell him to swear plain, or not at all. [Even]
- Turn to your mother. It is her duchy.

**You choose:** Tell him to swear plain, or not at all.

"Swear plain, my lord," you say, "or swear another day." Your voice does not go up at the end. Hervé looks at you for a long moment, as a man looks at a field he has been told is his neighbour's. Then he says the words again, plain, without the saving, and rises, and bows lower than he did before.

*The household: more loyal; Mahaut's respect: higher; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and slack trade: £710 4s 2d came in; the household £101 9s 2d, the men's pay £65 14s and repairs £35 went out*

### Penhoët's Claim

*Autumn 917, the third year of King Jehan*

After Michaelmas Hervé de Penhoët puts it in writing, before the council, in his own neat hand.

The duchy of Armance, he says, descended by the male line for three hundred years, until the war gave it to a woman. His house is the old line's nearest male heir. Your mother holds it for her life; he does not dispute that; he is a loyal man. But at her death, he says, it should come to Penhoët by the custom of the Armance, and not to her son, whose right comes to him through his mother, and the custom of the Armance has never counted a right that came through a woman. Her son is King of the West already, and has a crown to be going on with.

Then, at the bottom, under his seal, the offer. His daughter Sibylle is eight. If the King were to marry Sibylle, when they are both of age, the claim and the duchy would be in one bed, and there would be no quarrel to speak of, ever again.

"It's a good letter," says the chancellor, unhappily.

"It's an excellent letter," says your mother. "That is what I don't like about it."

- Hear the offer. Ask to meet Sibylle de Penhoët.
- Answer that the Armance is your mother's, and then yours, and there is nothing to discuss.
- Answer that the Estates will settle the law, and the claim with it.

**You choose:** Hear the offer. Ask to meet Sibylle de Penhoët.

Sibylle de Penhoët comes to court at Martinmas with her father: eight, solemn, her grandfather's chin and her father's patience. She curtsies to you, and to your mother, and then beats you at merels twice, and does not let you win the third game, which almost nobody at court would dare. You do not tell your mother that part.

*Penhoët: warmer*

### The Betrothal

*Winter 917, the third year of King Jehan*

In the dark of the year the council talks about your marriage, as it has every winter since you were crowned, and this year they talk about it in front of you, because you are ten, and a king unpromised at ten is a king every court in the world is writing letters about.

Your father would not promise you when the Valdrennish came. The offers have kept coming since. Sibylle de Penhoët, and the end of the Armance quarrel. Maëlle de Kerguen, Tanguy's cousin and ward, ten years old: Kerguen, the counterweight to Penhoët in the Armance, and Tanguy would like to see her a queen more than he can quite hide. And no one at all, as your mother was no one's until she chose.

Your mother says, "Whatever you choose, choose it yourself." Your father, in the garden, says, "Choose the one who'll still be there when it goes wrong."

- Betroth yourself to Sibylle de Penhoët.
- Betroth yourself to Maëlle de Kerguen.
- Promise yourself to no one. Not yet.

**You choose:** Betroth yourself to Maëlle de Kerguen.

Tanguy brings Maëlle to Lannec in the snow to be presented, and kneels to you himself, with great style, and stays to dinner, and makes your father laugh twice. Maëlle says nothing the whole evening, and at the end of it curtsies to you as if she meant it. Kerguen and the crown, against Penhoët and the custom of the Armance. Hervé hears of it at dinner and finishes his dinner.

*Penhoët: much colder; Tanguy: much warmer; Kerguen: much warmer; Kerguen's standing: much higher*

### The Easter Court

*Spring 918, the fourth year of King Jehan*

At Easter the court keeps the feast at Lannec, the long tables down the great hall, the boar's head, the Sarenzan musicians, and the whole West, or as much of it as owes you or wants something, in one hall.

Maëlle sits at your right hand as your betrothed, and Tanguy beside her tells stories, and Hervé de Penhoët sits exactly as far down the hall as his rank requires, and watches.

Your father sits at the side of the dais in his chair with arms. Halfway through the feast he gets up, slowly, with a hand on your shoulder, and calls for silence, and the hall gives it to him at once, the way it never quite does for you.

"A toast," he says.

- Let him make it.
- Stand, and make it with him. [Even]
- Before he speaks, send to Hervé and seat him at the high table.

**You choose:** Stand, and make it with him.

You stand beside him, and he hears you get up, and gives you the cup instead of raising it. "To the West," you say, "which made my father a king and my mother a queen and me a bother." The hall laughs, and drinks, and your father sits down beaming, and tells everyone for a week that he was interrupted by his own son at his own feast.

*David's shadow: shorter; Standing in the realm: higher*

### The Marsh

*Summer 918, the fourth year of King Jehan*

Bertrand de Quérec has been under guard at Lannec since the bridge, in a good room with a fire, waiting. The council has argued about him for two years. At midsummer your mother says the council will not decide it. You will. You are eleven.

You go to see him, once, before you decide. He gets up when you come in, and bows, which is not kneeling. "Well," he says. "You've grown."

- Pardon him, and give him the marsh, before the West.
- Send him over the march, and take his land for the crown.
- Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

**You choose:** Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

His son comes to court as your page, a boy of nine with his father's eyes, and is given a room near yours and a tutor and a pony, and every courtesy, and is never once let out of the walls of Lannec. Bertrand swears to you that autumn. He has to.

*Standing in the realm: higher; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and steady trade: £832 4s 2d came in; the household £118 17s 9d, the men's pay £65 14s and repairs £35 went out; Sir Geoffroi de Moncontour dies and leaves a son under age; the wardship of Moncontour is sold for £20*

### Aliénor

*Autumn 918, the fourth year of King Jehan*

Your sister Aliénor is seven, and the King's sister, and the most valuable girl in the West, and every great house has noticed. At Michaelmas the offers start coming for her too, as if she were a smaller crown.

Your mother says, "You'll have to decide what she is to you. Before someone else does."

- Keep her at court, at your side, where everyone can see.
- Send her to the Armance, to be your mother's.

**You choose:** Keep her at court, at your side, where everyone can see.

Aliénor gets the room next to yours, and a place at the high table on your left, and sits through council with you, kicking the footstool. Every envoy who comes to court about her hand has to get past her first, and she has opinions about all of them.

*Aliénor: much closer*

### The Regent

*Winter 918, the fourth year of King Jehan*

In the winter you are eleven, and you have sat through three years of council, and you have stopped kicking the footstool, and you have started, without meaning to, to disagree with your mother in front of people.

She notices. Of course she notices. At Candlemas she sends for you to her chamber, with the old nurse, and the fire, and the list she keeps of every lord in the West and what he wants.

"The regency ends when you are sixteen," she says. "I would like to spend the years until then teaching you how to rule, and not how to quarrel with me. You would like," and she smiles, a little, "to rule now. I know. I was eighteen when my grandmother died and the King made me his ward, and I would have killed for a vote in my own council."

"Then give me one."

- Ask for a seat in council with a vote of your own. [Even]
- Say you will learn from her, and mean it.
- Go to your father, and ask him to speak for you.

**You choose:** Ask for a seat in council with a vote of your own.

"No," she says, gently. "Not yet. Ask me again in a year, and ask me better." It is the right answer. You know it is the right answer, which makes it worse.

*David's shadow: longer*

### The King's Guard

*Summer 919, the fifth year of King Jehan*

At midsummer the captain of your guard dies, of a fever, at sixty: one of your father's old company, who carried you on his shoulders at your crowning so that you could see. The council has three names for his place.

His son, a solid, honest knight of thirty who has served in the guard since he was fifteen and has never once been surprised. An Armance knight of your mother's, Sire Guiomar, who held a gate at Mortefontaine with eleven men and is said never to have smiled since. And a young knight of your own choosing, twenty, a younger son with a good horse and no land, who has been teaching you to ride at the ring since the spring, and has never once let you win.

Davy Ludd says, "The son. He's dull. Dull's what you want at a door."

- The old captain's son. Your father's men keep your door.
- Sire Guiomar of the Armance. Your mother's men keep your door.
- The young knight. A king should choose his own.

**You choose:** The young knight. A king should choose his own.

He kneels to you on the tourney field in front of everyone, which was not the plan, and swears with a voice that cracks on "faith", and the whole court laughs, and he goes scarlet. By Michaelmas he has the guard turned out at dawn to ride at the ring with you, and the old company's sons have stopped laughing and started getting up early.

*David's shadow: much shorter; The household: less loyal*

### The Council of Works

*Summer 919, the fifth year of King Jehan*

Your mother gives the council a day at midsummer for the crown's works, and gives you the chair for it. Last Michaelmas brought a fair harvest and steady trade. The treasurer has his rolls, and the constable has his, and the Bishop of Saint-Lys has a letter.

"Choose, Your Grace," says your mother. "I'll tell you after if it was a mistake."

You have £7236 19s 4d.

- Rebuild the walls of Lannec. (£200)
- Build four cogs to carry the crown's salt to the south. (£250)
- Lend Kerguen a hundred pounds at ten in the hundred. (£100)
- Found a hospital on the pilgrims' road to Saint-Lys. (£60)
- Build the crown's granaries at Lannec and Sauvemer. (£80)

**You choose:** Charter a great fair at Lannec, for three weeks after Midsummer. (£150)

The first year there are cloth-men from Vervais and wine-men from the Midi and a bear. By the third, the Sarenzan bankers have a booth, and the crown's clerk at the gate takes a penny on every bale.

*Coin −£150; Standing with the merchants: much higher; You hold: The great fair at Lannec (£15 a year)*

### The Salt Towns' Charter

*Autumn 919, the fifth year of King Jehan*

At Michaelmas the burgesses of the salt towns come up the river to Lannec, six of them in good wool, with a charter already written out on the best parchment in the West, wanting only a seal.

It asks for the right to choose their own mayors, keep their own weights and scales, and pay the crown a fixed farm every Michaelmas instead of whatever the crown's men can find. Your father remitted their arrears, and they have not forgotten it. This is the next thing.

"Two hundred pounds a year," says the eldest of them, "in silver, for ever. Your Grace's father's men never got a hundred and fifty out of us in a good year."

The chancellor says the towns will be harder to tax in a bad year. Your mother says the towns will be harder to frighten in a bad year, which is not the same thing, and looks at you.

- Seal the charter.
- Seal it for three hundred, and see if they'll pay. [Even]
- Tell them a charter for ever is a matter for the Estates.

**You choose:** Seal the charter.

You seal it yourself, with the Great Seal, which takes both hands. The burgesses kneel, and pay the first year's farm on the spot, in silver, out of a chest they brought up the river on the chance. The chancellor counts it twice. It is all there.

*Coin +£20; Standing in the realm: higher; The household: less loyal*

### Before the Estates

*Spring 920, the sixth year of King Jehan*

At Lady Day the summons goes out under the Great Seal, in your name, though you did not write it: the Estates of the West will sit at Lannec at Whitsun to settle the law of succession of the crown.

Your father promised the Estates, in the first year of his reign, that they would choose the West's next king. Then he crowned you in his lifetime, and they never chose at all. Now they will decide whether the crown of the West is theirs to give each time it falls, or whether it goes by blood, and if by blood, whose. You are thirteen. You are the case.

Hervé de Penhoët comes to court before the summons is a week old, himself, with his hat in his hands. "I'll vote for Your Grace's crown," he says. "The West has a king, and I'm a loyal man. I ask only that Your Grace let the Estates write the custom of the Armance as it was: by the male line, and through men."

Your mother comes to your chamber that night. "He is offering you your crown," she says, "for my duchy. For your duchy, after me." She sits on the end of your bed, as she did when you were six. "I'll not tell you what to do. I'll tell you that I held it, and my grandmother held it, and I would like you to hold it after me."

In the garden, in the morning, your father says, "Well. You're the King."

You put the two answers side by side, and look at them, and do not give either. Not yet.

- Go on.

**You choose:** Go on.

### The End

Here Act I of Book One ends. You are Jehan, King of the West, thirteen, under your mother's regency, with your father in the garden, betrothed to Maëlle de Kerguen, and the Estates summoned to decide whether the crown of the West is theirs to give.

Act II, The Law, is still being written.

### The King at Lannec

*Spring 912, the seventh year of King Hob*

The great hall at Lannec is cold in the mornings, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the dais yet, and the chamberlain stands at your elbow with the day's petitions under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

You were a reeve's son once. Now the hall stands when you come in, and the hall is the hall where the Estates of the West made you king, and every morning you walk past the place where you stood that day and do not look at it.

Mahaut comes down with her women. She is thirty-one, and Queen of the West, and Duchess of Armance in her own right, which she has never once let a clerk forget. She takes the chair beside yours and moves the salt so you can reach it. Ysolde comes in late from the mews, nineteen, with mud on her boots and a hawk's jesses still round her wrist, and the small crown you set on her head at Lannec pushed back on her hair like a hat. She kisses your hand, and bows to Mahaut, which is not the same thing, and sits. Mahaut's own children come behind with their nurse.

"Your daughter," says Mahaut, "has been in the stables again." She has never once said "our".

"My daughter," you say, "is Queen of the West. She can be in the stables if she likes."

"That," says Mahaut, "is what I am afraid of."

- Ride out to the fields with Ysolde.
- Go through the treasurer's rolls before the petitioners come.
- Hear the petitions in the great hall yourself.

**You choose:** Ride out to the fields with Ysolde.

You ride out by the river gate side by side, and Ysolde rides better than you do now, and pretends not to notice. The people on the road stop and bow, to her first. "They know who's next," she says. "They know who'll be paying," you say, and she laughs, and races you to the ford, and lets you win, badly.

### Quérec

The summer court ends with the oaths, as it does every year: the lords of the West renew their faith to the King, and this year, for the first time, you have asked them to swear to Ysolde as your heir.

### The Illness

*Winter 914, the ninth year of King Hob*

It comes at Christmas, with the great hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there. Mahaut is sitting beside it in her furs with a book of accounts on her knee, and turns a page, and looks up when you move, and you see on her face what you look like. Ysolde is asleep in the chair by the window, in her riding clothes, with a dispatch from the march on her knee.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse.

"Well," says Davy, from the door. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"There is a Sarenzan physician in the town," says Mahaut, "who cured a bishop. There is the relic of Saint Gildas at the abbey. And there is lying still and doing what you're told." She closes the book. "I would choose the third. You won't."

- Send for the Sarenzan. (£10) [Even]
- Have the saint's finger brought from the abbey. [Even]

**You choose:** Lie still and do as Mahaut says, for once.

You lie still. Mahaut runs the kingdom from a chair beside your bed for the whole of Lent, and runs it well, and tells you about it every evening whether you want to hear or not. It is the first winter in thirty years you have not ridden out. The West, you notice, does not fall down.

### The Handover

*Spring 915, the tenth year of King Hob*

Lady Day, the new year. You sit in the gallery in a chair with a fur over your knees, and you know.

You will live. The physicians say so, and you believe them, and you can feel it: you will sit in the sun for years yet. But you will not ride to the march again, or sit a whole day's assize, or hold a great council by the force of standing up in it. The kingdom cannot wait for you to get better. It needs someone to rule it now.

You crowned Ysolde beside you years ago, so that there would never be a day without a crowned head. She has worn the crown for show since, at feasts and Easter courts. Now she must wear it for use: the seals, the council, the march, all of it, while you sit in the sun. She is twenty-two, and needs no regent. You would be there, behind the throne, for as long as you last. Mahaut would be there too: the Queen, and Duchess of Armance in her own right, and the mother of Jehan, who will have the Armance after her. She will be Ysolde's counsellor, or her rival. She has not decided which, and neither has Ysolde.

The Estates have sworn to her. That will help. Bishop Évrard of Saint-Lys, who crowned you, is a hundred and one, and has sent word that he would like to see the thing done properly, in his cathedral, if it is soon.

- Give her the rule at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.
- Give her the seals quietly in the palace chapel, and tell the West after.
- Give her the rule, and name her council in the same hour, so no one else can.

**You choose:** Give her the rule at Saint-Lys, before the Estates and all the West.

They carry you into the cathedral in a chair, and you stand for it, because you will not sit. You take the Great Seal off its chain and put it in Ysolde's hands, before the Estates and the altar, and old Évrard blesses it with hands that shake so badly two canons have to steady them, and the nave roars. Ysolde does not cry, and does not smile, and looks at the seal for a long moment, as if it were heavier than the crown.

## The Heir

### The Council

*Summer 915, the first year of Queen Ysolde*

You are twenty-two, and Queen of the West in more than name now, and the chair at the head of the council table was made for your father and still has the shape of him in the cushion.

Your stepmother sits at the other end of the table. She is not your regent; you have none. She is the Queen, and Duchess of Armance, and the Armance lords sit on her side of the table and look at her before they vote. Her son Jehan sits at her elbow, being taught to listen. The chancellor calls you "Your Grace", every time, as if checking it still works.

Your father is in his rooms along the gallery. He is better. He sits in the sun in the mornings, and you go and see him after council, and he asks you what they said, and laughs.

Today the council is talking about Quérec. He swore to you, and kissed your hand, and has sent a letter congratulating your stepmother on her stepdaughter's rule, addressed to her and not to you. ### The Queen's Council

*Autumn 915, the first year of Queen Ysolde*

After Michaelmas your stepmother sends for you to her own chamber, with only her old nurse present, the way she once sent for your father when they were deciding something nobody could know yet.

"The council," she says, "is your father's men, and my men, and they hate each other quietly, which is the best kind of council. I want to change it." She puts a list in front of you. ### The Marsh

*Winter 915, the first year of Queen Ysolde*

At Candlemas the oath Bertrand swore in the great hall, with your father's guard along the wall, turns out to have been worth what such oaths are worth. He takes the crown's salt-works at the edge of the marsh, and the toll bridge, and says in a letter to every lord in the West that he swore to a King, not to a King's daughter and a Queen who is no kin of hers.

His father's old tenants have risen for him, and the marsh lords who remember his father. Forty marsh lords are with him, and two hundred men, and a ship from Valdrenne has been seen off the salt flats, though nobody can say whose.

The council meets for three days. The constable wants to march at once, in the frost. The chancellor wants to treat. Your stepmother listens to both and says nothing, which you have learned means she has decided and is waiting to see if anyone agrees.

On the third day she turns to you. "It is your crown he has hung on that bridge," she says. "What do you want?"

- Treat with him. Offer a pardon for the bridge.

**You choose:** March, and ride with the host yourself, under your own banner.

You ride at the head of six hundred men in the frost, in your father's old harness, with the constable at your left hand saying nothing useful. At the toll bridge the marsh men see the queen's own banner coming over the causeway and lose their nerve, half of them, before anyone has drawn. Bertrand holds the tower for two days, and then asks for terms, and you give them yourself, on the causeway, with your helmet under your arm. ### The King's Question

*Spring 916, the second year of Queen Ysolde*

In the spring your father is well enough to sit in the garden at the foot of the palace wall, in the sun, with a blanket and a cup of wine he is not supposed to have.

You sit with him most mornings, after council. Today he talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from: a reeve's son, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About your own mother, whom he married when he had one horse, and who died when your brother was born, and whom he has not named aloud in years. About Mahaut, after, and the garden at Lannec. About the war, and the bridge, and the Estates, and the morning they made him king and he stood in the hall and could not feel his own feet.

Then he stops, and looks at you, and you can see him decide to say it.

"You're twenty-three," he says. "You've had a year of it. Bridges and councils and your stepmother." He takes your hand. His is dry and light. "Tell me. Are you going to be me?"

He laughs, which hurts him, and grips your hand. "Good," he says. "Good. One of me was plenty."

## Book One: The Keeper

### The Homage of the Lords

*Summer 917, the third year of Queen Ysolde*

At midsummer the great lords of the West come to Lannec to do you homage in person, now that the Estates have acclaimed you: every man who holds of the crown, one by one, on his knees, with his hands between yours.

Your hands are your father's, broad, with a rider's calluses. The lords' hands are older. By the twentieth you have stopped noticing whose they are, and by the fortieth your stepmother, at your right hand, has started saying the names under her breath before each man reaches the dais: she knows every one of them, and you know most, and you would rather she did not know that you know.

Hervé de Penhoët comes near the end. He is a careful man of fifty in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword. He kneels, and puts his hands between yours, and says the words, all of them, clearly, and then three more.

"Saving the right of my house," he says, "in the duchy of Armance."

Beside you, your stepmother has gone completely still.

- Take his homage, saving and all, and say nothing.
- Tell him to swear plain, or not at all. [Even]
- Turn to your stepmother. It is her duchy.

**You choose:** Tell him to swear plain, or not at all.

"Swear plain, my lord," you say, "or swear another day." Your voice does not go up at the end. Hervé looks at you for a long moment, as a man looks at a field he has been told is his neighbour's. Then he says the words again, plain, without the saving, and rises, and bows lower than he did before.

*The household: more loyal; Mahaut's respect: higher; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and slack trade: £699 3s 4d came in; the household £99 17s 7d, the men's pay £20 14s and repairs £40 went out*

### Penhoët's Claim

*Autumn 917, the third year of Queen Ysolde*

After Michaelmas Hervé de Penhoët puts it in writing, before the council, in his own neat hand.

The duchy of Armance, he says, descended by the male line for three hundred years, until the war gave it to a woman. His house is the old line's nearest male heir. Your stepmother holds it for her life; he does not dispute that; he is a loyal man. But at her death, he says, it should come to Penhoët by the custom of the Armance, and not to her son Jehan, whose right comes to him through his mother, and the custom of the Armance has never counted a right that came through a woman. The Queen is no kin of the Armance at all, and has a crown to be going on with.

Then, at the bottom, under his seal, the offer. His son Ronan is twenty-one. If the Queen were to marry Ronan, the crown and Penhoët would be one house, and the Armance's quarrel a family matter, to be settled at a family table, by the queen.

"It's a good letter," says the chancellor, unhappily.

"It's an excellent letter," says your stepmother. "That is what I don't like about it."

- Hear the offer. Ask to meet Ronan de Penhoët.
- Answer that the Armance is your stepmother's, and then Jehan's, and there is nothing to discuss.
- Answer that the Estates will settle the law, and the claim with it.

**You choose:** Hear the offer. Ask to meet Ronan de Penhoët.

Ronan de Penhoët comes to court at Martinmas: tall, quiet, his grandfather's shoulders and his father's patience, and none of either's habit of saying the clever thing. He bows to you, and to your stepmother, and talks to you for an hour about horses as if you were a person, which almost nobody at court does. You do not tell your stepmother that part.

*Penhoët: warmer*

### The Betrothal

*Winter 917, the third year of Queen Ysolde*

In the dark of the year the council talks about your marriage, as it has every winter since you were crowned, and this year they talk about it in front of you, because you are twenty-four, and a queen unpromised at twenty-four is a queen every court in the world is writing letters about.

Your father would not promise you when the Valdrennish came. The offers have kept coming since. Ronan de Penhoët, and the end of the Armance quarrel. Tanguy de Kerguen, the counterweight to Penhoët in the Armance, who is twenty-six and would like to be a king's consort more than he can quite hide. And no one at all, as your stepmother was no one's until she chose.

Your stepmother says, "Whatever you choose, choose it yourself." Your father, in the garden, says, "Choose the one who'll still be there when it goes wrong."

- Betroth yourself to Ronan de Penhoët.
- Betroth yourself to Tanguy de Kerguen.
- Promise yourself to no one. Not yet.

**You choose:** Betroth yourself to Tanguy de Kerguen.

Tanguy rides to Lannec in the snow to kneel to you, and does it with great style, and stays to dinner, and makes your father laugh twice. Kerguen and the crown, against Penhoët and the custom of the Armance. Hervé hears of it at dinner and finishes his dinner.

*Penhoët: much colder; Tanguy: much warmer; Kerguen: much warmer; Kerguen's standing: much higher*

### The Easter Court

*Spring 918, the fourth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Easter the court keeps the feast at Lannec, the long tables down the great hall, the boar's head, the Sarenzan musicians, and the whole West, or as much of it as owes you or wants something, in one hall.

Tanguy sits at your right hand as your betrothed, and tells stories, and Hervé de Penhoët sits exactly as far down the hall as his rank requires, and watches.

Your father sits at the side of the dais in his chair with arms. Halfway through the feast he gets up, slowly, with a hand on your shoulder, and calls for silence, and the hall gives it to him at once, the way it never quite does for you.

"A toast," he says.

- Let him make it.
- Stand, and make it with him. [Even]
- Before he speaks, send to Hervé and seat him at the high table.

**You choose:** Stand, and make it with him.

You stand, and the hall turns to look, and your father's toast stops in the middle, and the moment goes out of the room like air from a bladder. He finishes it, kindly. Nobody remembers what either of you said.

*Hob's shadow: longer*

### The Marsh

*Summer 918, the fourth year of Queen Ysolde*

Bertrand de Quérec has been under guard at Lannec since the bridge, in a good room with a fire, waiting. The council has argued about him for two years. At midsummer your stepmother says the council will not decide it. You will. You are twenty-five.

You go to see him, once, before you decide. He gets up when you come in, and bows, which is not kneeling. "Well," he says. "You've grown."

- Pardon him, and give him the marsh, before the West.
- Send him over the march, and take his land for the crown.
- Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

**You choose:** Keep his land in his hands, and his son in yours.

His son comes to court as your page, a boy of nine with his father's eyes, and is given a room near yours and a tutor and a pony, and every courtesy, and is never once let out of the walls of Lannec. Bertrand swears to you that autumn. He has to.

### Piers

*Autumn 918, the fourth year of Queen Ysolde*

Your brother Piers is twenty-three. He is the King's son, and you are the King's daughter, and since the day you were crowned there have been men in the West who say so in that order.

At Michaelmas you hear it said aloud. At the Penhoët table, at a hunting dinner, a Brésy cousin raises his cup "to the King's son", and nobody at the table corrects him. It comes back to you in a day, the way everything does.

Your stepmother says, "You'll have to decide what he is to you. Before someone else does."

- Keep him at court, at your side, where everyone can see.
- Give him to the Church. A bishop's mitre is a crown too.
- Send him to the Armance, to be your stepmother's.

**You choose:** Keep him at court, at your side, where everyone can see.

*Piers: much closer*

### The Queen's Son

*Winter 918, the fourth year of Queen Ysolde*

In the winter you are twenty-five, and have ruled for three years, and have started, without meaning to, to disagree with your stepmother in front of people.

She notices. At Candlemas she sends for you to her chamber, with the fire, and the list she keeps of every lord in the West and what he wants.

"I am not your mother," she says. "We both know it, and we have both been very polite about it for years. I have a son. Jehan is a child still, and the Armance is mine, and after me it is his, by my grandmother's will. Penhoët says the custom does not count a right that comes through a woman. The crown could say the same, one day, if it wanted the Armance for itself." She puts a parchment on the table between you: a confirmation of Jehan's right, under the Great Seal, with a space at the foot for your name. "I would like you to be the queen who signed it."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I will know," she says, "and so will the Armance."

- Sign it, and carry it through the council yourself. [Even]
- Tell her the Armance is hers to settle, and that you will not stand in her way.
- Go to your father, and ask him what he promised her.

**You choose:** Sign it, and carry it through the council yourself.

You sign it at her table, and carry it into council the next morning, and make your father's men vote for it one by one, which takes until Sext. When it is sealed your stepmother takes it, and reads it twice, and says, "Thank you, Your Grace," which is the first time she has ever called you that in private.

*Mahaut's respect: higher; Hob's shadow: shorter; Penhoët: much colder*

### The Queen's Guard

*Summer 919, the fifth year of Queen Ysolde*

At midsummer the captain of your guard dies, of a fever, at sixty: one of your father's old company, who stood at the cathedral door the day your father gave you the seals. The council has three names for his place.

His son, a solid, honest knight of thirty who has served in the guard since he was fifteen and has never once been surprised. An Armance knight of your stepmother's, Sire Guiomar, who held a gate at Mortefontaine with eleven men and is said never to have smiled since. And a young knight of your own choosing, twenty, a younger son with a good horse and no land, who has been at every tourney you have watched this year and has never once looked at the stands.

By Michaelmas the court has stopped laughing and started gossiping, and he still has not once looked at the stands.

*Hob's shadow: much shorter; The household: less loyal*

### A Letter from Cordelle

*Autumn 919, the fifth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Michaelmas a letter comes for you from Cordelle, from King Amaury of Valdrenne, not to the council, not to your stepmother: to you, under his privy seal, in his own hand.

It is a kind letter. It says that he too came to a crown while an old man still lived who thought it his, and that he remembers how long those years were. It asks how you do, and what you hunt, and whether your council lets you read your own letters yet. The messenger is waiting in the gallery for an answer. He has been told to give it to no one but you.

### Before the Estates

*Spring 920, the sixth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Lady Day the summons goes out under the Great Seal, in your name: the Estates of the West will sit at Lannec at Whitsun to settle the law of succession of the crown. You wrote it yourself, and the chancellor corrected your Latin.

Nobody has ever written it down. The crown went to you because your father put it on your head. The Estates will decide whether that was a law, or a favour. You are twenty-seven. You are the case.

Hervé de Penhoët comes to court before the summons is a week old, himself, with his hat in his hands. "I'll vote for Your Grace's crown," he says. "The West has a queen, and I'm a loyal man. I ask only that Your Grace let the Estates write the custom of the Armance as it was: by the male line."

Your stepmother comes to your chamber that night, and stands at the window; she has never sat on your bed, and never will. "He is offering you your crown," she says, "for my son's duchy. I'll not tell you what to do. I'll tell you that I held it, and my grandmother held it, and I would like Jehan to hold it after me. I would like you to help him."

In the garden, in the morning, your father says, "Well. You're the Queen."

You put the two answers side by side, and look at them, and do not give either. Not yet.

- Go on.

**You choose:** Go on.

## Book One, Act Two: The Law

> A crown is a promise the Estates made once, and are asked to make again every time it changes heads.

### The Estates Sit

*Summer 920, the sixth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Whitsun in 920 the Estates of the West sit at Lannec, in the hall where your father was made king and you were acclaimed: the lords on their benches, the bishops in their stalls, the burgesses of the salt towns at the back. They have the law of succession in front of them, and a petition from the male-line lords to write it again.

Before the first session Hervé de Penhoët asks for an hour, and gets it, in your stepmother's solar, with your stepmother in the room. He says it again, plainly: Penhoët's voice, and the voices of the Armance lords who go with Penhoët, for the queen's cause. In return, the custom of the Armance, written by the Estates as it was: by the male line. At your stepmother's death the duchy would come to Penhoët, and not to her son Jehan.

Your stepmother says nothing at all. She is looking at you.

- Take Hervé's bargain. The crown is worth a duchy.
- Refuse him. The Armance is your stepmother's, and then Jehan's.
- Tell him the Estates will write the Armance's custom, not the crown.

**You choose:** Refuse him. The Armance is your stepmother's, and then Jehan's.

Hervé bows, as if you had given him the answer he expected, and says that Penhoët is a patient house. He votes against the crown that week, and so do the Armance lords who go with him. He says nothing about the duchy again. Your stepmother says, that night, "He'll wait for me to die. Let him wait."

### The Crown's Case

*Autumn 920, the sixth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Michaelmas the male-line lords present their petition: the crown and the great fiefs of the West to pass by sons, and through sons, as Valdrenne's do, "for the next reign, if not for this one". For this one too, some of them say, more quietly. The council meets to decide what the crown will answer.

- Answer the petition yourself, in the hall. [Even]
- Let your stepmother answer it, as Duchess of Armance.

**You choose:** Let the Estates decide it without the crown.

### The Wet Summer

*Summer 921, the seventh year of Queen Ysolde*

It rains from Midsummer to Lammas, every day, warm rain that lies in the furrows and rots the wheat in the ear. *Summer 923, the ninth year of Queen Ysolde*

At midsummer the free companies come down from the north, paid off from Caldmoor's war and looking for the next one, and Bertrand de Quérec finds them work.

## Book One, Act Three: The Children's Mortality

> In the second Mottle the old buried the young, which is the wrong way round.

Tanguy de Kerguen is at the front of the nave in a doublet that cost more than a manor, and makes the whole feast laugh, and makes your father laugh twice.

- Go on to the feast, and the bed.

**You choose:** Go on to the feast.

The feast lasts three days. On the third, a ship's master from Sauvemer, the same who brought the wine at Easter, asks to see the chancellor, and is a long time with him.

*Ysolde marries Tanguy de Kerguen; Kerguen: much warmer; Kerguen's standing: much higher*

### The Sickness Comes

*Summer 925, the eleventh year of Queen Ysolde*

The ship's master's sickness in Sarenza is in Sauvemer by Midsummer, in the harbour quarter first, in the children first. By Saint John's Eve there are carts in the streets at night and pits outside the walls. By Lammas it is in Lannec.

Children, the physicians say, the young ones worst, as if the Mottle had come back for what it missed. The court is in the palace. The council is in the council chamber. Your stepmother has had the windows shut and the doors of the nursery nailed. Your father, in his chair by the fire, says, "I saw the first one. This one is different. It wants the young."

### The Old King

*Spring 926, the twelfth year of Queen Ysolde*

In Lent of 926 your father takes the Mottle. It comes on him in the night, the fever and the dark mottling of the skin, and in the morning the physicians come out of his chamber and will not look at you.

### The Crown in the Plague

*Summer 926, the twelfth year of Queen Ysolde*

### Black on Penhoët's Gate

*Autumn 927, the thirteenth year of Queen Ysolde*

### The Count of the Dead

*Spring 928, the fourteenth year of Queen Ysolde*

At Lady Day in 928 the sickness lifts, as the first one did, all at once and for no reason anyone can give. The bells stop. The pits outside the walls are filled in and sown with grass.

### The Crown After the Mottle

*Spring 929, the fifteenth year of Queen Ysolde*

In the spring of 929 the West counts what the Mottle left it. ### The Queen's Illness

At Candlemas in 934 your stepmother falls ill at her castle in the Armance hills: a wasting, the physicians say, and the kind that takes a year. She is fifty-three. The Armance lords ride to her gate to be seen there, and so do Penhoët's men, who are counted by her porter and leave their names.

### The End

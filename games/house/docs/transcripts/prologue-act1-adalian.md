## Prologue: The Old Lord

> A house is three generations: the man who makes it, the one who keeps it, and the one who spends it.

### The Hall

*Spring 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

The hall is cold in the mornings now, and so are you. You are forty-nine. The fire was lit at Prime and has not reached the high table yet, and the steward stands at your elbow with the tallies under his arm, waiting for you to be ready.

The West is Adalia's. The lords voted it at Lannec eight years ago, and King Edwin rules it from Wendmere through the King's governor at Lannec. Governors come and go. The taxes stay. You were a younger son of nobody in particular once. Now you hold Kerval, and men stand when you come in.

The family comes down to break their fast. Agnès takes the place on your right, as she has for years, and moves the salt so you can reach it. John sits below you, twenty, and eats like someone with somewhere to be. Robert sits beside him and steals from his trencher, the way he has since he could reach.

"The reeve's here about the lower field," says the steward. "And there's a man from Penhoët at the gate. He won't say what he wants. He says he'll wait."

"Let him wait," you say. "He's a Penhoët. They're good at it."

- Go over the books with the steward while the fire catches.

**You choose:** Ride the bounds with John before the reeve gets you.

You ride out by the mill with John at your stirrup, and show him the stones: the one your men set, the one somebody moved, the one nobody can remember. "That one," you say, "is worth a lawsuit. Remember where it is." He looks at it as if it might get up and walk.

### The Old Company

*Spring 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

Piers atte Brook comes up from the yard after Terce, the way he has every day since you had a yard. He has gone grey at the temples and bald on top, and he walks with one shoulder lower than the other, from a thing at a bridge you were both too young to be at.

"Your Penhoët's gone," he says. "Left a letter. I told him you'd read it when you'd eaten. He said he'd heard you were a man of appetite."

"And what did you say?"

"I said you'd heard he was a man of letters." He sits without asking, which nobody else in the hall would do, and pours himself your ale. "There's fewer of us at table every Christmas. You've noticed."

You have. You can count the old company on your hands now and have fingers over: the ones who rode with you when you had nothing, who held the line when holding it was stupid, who stood behind you at Lannec. The rest are names you say when the wine is good.

"I'm not asking for anything," says Piers. "I want that said first."

"Then you're the only man in the West who isn't."

"I didn't say I didn't want anything." He turns the cup. "I want to know what happens to us. After."

- Give him a holding of his own, with a hearth he can die at.
- Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.
- Keep him at your side to the end. You will need someone who remembers.

**You choose:** Ask him to stand at John's shoulder when you are gone.

"John?" He laughs. "He'll not want an old man telling him how you did it." "No," you say. "That's why." He thinks about it, and nods once, and finishes the ale like a man signing something.

### The Heir

*Summer 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

Haymaking, and the whole manor out in the long meadow with the sun on its neck. You find John at the edge of it, watching the work with his arms folded, counting the cocks of hay and frowning at the ones that are too small.

### Penhoët's Suit

*Summer 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

The letter the Penhoët man left is a writ, and it is sealed with the Penhoët boar. Hervé de Penhoët sues you before the King's justices for Kerval, which, he says, was held by his grandfather and taken from his house without right.

He comes himself a week later, which you did not expect. He is a careful man of forty-five in good dark wool, with a clerk behind him and no sword, and he takes his hat off in your hall and holds it in both hands.

"My father is dying," he says. "He'd like to see it settled before he goes. So would I. I don't want it said I waited for him to die to bring it."

"You've brought it now."

"I've brought it to you first." He nods at your steward, who has gone grey in the face. "Your man's looking for a charter. I hope he finds it. I'd rather lose to a good charter than win against a bad one. It saves us both the next forty years."

"And if he doesn't find it?"

"Then I'll win," says Hervé, as if he is telling you the weather, "and we'll still have to live next to each other."

- Buy him off. Name a sum and see if he is a man of business. (£30)

**You choose:** Fight it at law, on the old charter.

The steward finds a copy of a copy, in a hand nobody knows, with a hole where the witnesses should be. "It'll do," he says, in the voice of a man who knows it will not. Hervé's clerk asks, politely, whether he might see it, and makes a note.

### The First Michaelmas

*Autumn 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

Michaelmas, and the reckoning. The steward sets up his table in the hall with the counters and the cloth, and the reeves come in one by one with their tallies and their excuses, and the bailiffs behind them with the rents in leather bags.

You sit through all of it. Your father did not have a steward or a cloth or anything to count, and you still feel it, the weight of the bags, when they are put down in front of you. The rents are in, most of them. The men want paying. The roof of the long barn wants mending before the winter, and the smith wants his fifteen shillings, again.

### Penhoët's Offer

*Winter 912, the eleventh year of King Edwin*

A letter comes at Martinmas, in a shaking hand you have not seen for twenty years: Yann de Penhoët would like to see you before he dies, and will you come. It is an old house, older than yours by three hundred years, with its towers patched in newer stone and the boar over every door.

Yann is in bed in the great chamber with a fire built up like a forge. He is seventy, and he was a big man, and the bones are still big under the blanket.

"You," he says. "Look at you. Grey as me." He coughs for a while, and waves at the wine. "You stood against me at Lannec. I stood against you. We were both wrong, I expect. I'm too old to remember which way."

You pour him wine. He does not drink it.

"Kerval was my grandfather's," he says. "I'll die thinking it. Hervé will live thinking it. And one day his son will want it back, and your son will say no, and some boy will get his head broken over a mill." He looks at you. "I'd like it finished. My grandson Ronan is sixteen. You've a child of an age. Make a marriage, let the mill be the dowry, and let it be finished."

**You choose:** Say you will put it to Robert before you answer.

"Ask," says Yann, and laughs until he coughs. "My father never asked me. I'd have said no." At home Robert hears all of it, says he will think, and goes out to the stables to do it. By Candlemas he says yes, to your surprise and possibly to his own.

### The Illness

*Winter 914, the thirteenth year of King Edwin*

It comes at Christmas, with the hall full and the boar's head on the table. A shaking first, that you put down to the cold. Then a heat, and a weight on your chest like a man kneeling on it.

You wake in your own bed with no memory of getting there, and John asleep in a chair with his mouth open. Agnès is at the window, and turns when you move, and you see on her face what you look like.

The fever goes in a week. The weakness does not. You cannot get up the stairs without stopping. You cannot sit a horse. You never thought about your body, except when it was bleeding, and now you think about nothing else.

"Well," says Piers atte Brook, sitting on the end of the bed. "You look like a thing the dogs dug up."

"Thank you."

"There's a Sarenzan physician at Lannec who's cured a bishop. There's Saint Gildas's finger at the abbey. And there's lying still and doing what you're told, which you've never tried."

**You choose:** Send to Lannec for the Sarenzan. (£10)

The Sarenzan bleeds you, purges you, and charges you ten pounds. You are weaker after him than before. He says it is the disease leaving. Piers says it is ten pounds leaving.

## The Heir

> The son of a great man has two fathers: the one he had, and the one everybody else remembers.

### First Days

*Summer 915, the fourteenth year of King Edwin*

The chair is too big. Nobody says so. It was made for your father, and the arms are worn pale where his hands went, and when you sit in it your own hands do not reach the same places.

Your father is upstairs. You can hear him cough through the floor at night, and in the day he sends down questions by the chamber servants: has the reeve been, what did the smith say, is the long barn roofed.

On the fourth morning Piers atte Brook brings you the first thing to sign. It is an order for the garrison: twelve men at the gate, day and night, as your father always had it. It costs as much as the long barn.

"Your father's way," Piers atte Brook says, and holds out the pen, and waits. So does the hall. You can feel it waiting, all the way to the kitchens.

### The Will

*Autumn 915, the fourteenth year of King Edwin*

After Michaelmas your father sends for the chaplain and for you, and dictates a will from the bed, a sentence at a time, with long stops for breath. You sit by the window and listen to your father give away your house.

Most of it is what you expected: the old company provided for, the Masses, the chaplain's horse. Then Robert. A manor of his own, the mill-farm at the ford and the land that goes with it, a third of what the house holds south of the river. And his Penhoët marriage to be kept, whatever comes.

It is generous. It is the kind of thing people do when they are dying and have a child they were hard on. It is a third of the land south of the river.

"Well?" says your father. His voice is thin. His eyes are not.

- Honour it, every line.
- Ask him to change it, to your face. [Even]

**You choose:** Ask him to change it, to your face.

You say it plainly: the house cannot carry it, and the house is what he built. Your father is angry, then quiet, then angry again. In the end he says, "Half, then. Half, and you tell them it was my idea." It is the closest to a compliment he has paid you since you were twelve.

### The Deathbed

*Spring 916, the fifteenth year of King Edwin*

Your father is dying at Lady Day, and knows it, and has the shutters open so he can hear the yard. The chaplain has been and gone. Agnès sits on the far side of the bed with her hands in her lap. The old company is on the stairs, taking turns.

He talks. He has not talked like this in your life. About where he came from, with nothing, in a year nobody remembers. About the first manor, and the first winter, and the men who stayed.

### The Oath

*Spring 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

Lady Day, 917. You ride to Lannec to do homage for your father's lands, which are yours.

## Book One: The Keeper

### The Homage of the Knights

*Summer 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

They come at midsummer, as they came to your father every year: the knights who hold land of the house, riding in by twos and threes with their squires and their best horses and their oldest grievances. Sir Gautier d'Aubrac, Sir Alain de Coatmen and Sir Renaud de Saint-Aubin, and the rest behind them.

The hall is hung with the house's colours. The chair at the end of it is yours. You sit in it with your hands on the arms where your father's hands went, and they come up one by one, and kneel, and put their hands between yours.

"I become your man of the lands I hold of you," says the first, an old knight with a white beard and a voice like a cart on gravel, "as I was your father's." He says the last part quietly, as if it is between the two of you.

Then the next, and the next. By the sixth your hands are cold from theirs. The steward stands at your shoulder with the roll, and reads out each man's service as he rises: forty days in war, suit of court, a horse at Michaelmas, a pair of gilt spurs at Easter, as it was in your father's day.

- Take every homage on your father's terms, word for word.
- Change the terms as they kneel. Lighter dues, more days in the field. [Even]
- Ask each man what he wants of you, before he swears. [Even]

**You choose:** Ask each man what he wants of you, before he swears.

They tell you, at length, every one of them. By None you have agreed to three things you cannot afford, and the old knight with the white beard is asleep on the bench by the door.

*Coin −£5*

### The Knight Who Did Not Kneel

*Summer 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

The last of them does not kneel.

Sir Renaud de Saint-Aubin stands in front of the chair with his hat in his hand, a lean man in a good cloak, and looks at you with the face of someone who has rehearsed this on the road.

"I held of your father," he says. "I'd like to see what you are before I put my hands between yours. No offence meant. I knelt to a man I knew."

The hall has gone quiet. Somebody's dog yawns.

- Release him from his homage, and let him go.
- Hold him to the oath his land owes, here, in front of them all. [Favorable]
- Answer him plainly, and let him choose.

**You choose:** Hold him to the oath his land owes, here, in front of them all.

"You hold your land of this house," you say, "whoever sits in this chair. Kneel, or give it back." For a long breath he does nothing. Then he goes down on one knee, and then the other, and puts his hands between yours, and says the words. His hands are colder than yours. Behind you somebody lets out a breath they have been holding since Terce.

*The household: more loyal; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and slack trade: £240 5s came in; the household £24 6d, the men's pay £9 and repairs £20 went out*

### The New Governor

*Autumn 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

At Michaelmas a new King's governor at Lannec comes up the valley with forty men, to see the March for himself. He is younger than the last one and has a sharper collar, and he has brought the founder's patent with him in a box, and the old governor's notes on it.

"Your father defended every line of this," he says, "and won. I've read his answers. They're good." He does not say it as a compliment.

He wants the West's liberties read narrowly: the right to be tried by the West's own courts, the right to refuse service over the Narrow Sea. He wants them read as favours, not as rights.

- Concede the reading he wants, and keep the King's goodwill.
- Stand on the patent as it is written.
- Write to Wendmere over his head. [Even]

**You choose:** Stand on the patent as it is written.

"It says what it says," you tell him. "Father didn't fight for favours." He writes that down, word for word, and reads it back to you to make sure. It goes to Wendmere by the next courier.

*The sovereign's favour: lower*

### The Year's End

*Autumn 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

The year turns. The roads freeze. In the long evenings the hall is full of people who want to talk to you about one thing, and they all approach it sideways.

The thing is your marriage. You are twenty-five, and unmarried, and the head of a great house, and every family in the West with a daughter or a son of an age has begun to send you wine.

- Let them talk.

**You choose:** Let them talk.

### The Keeper's Match

*Winter 917, the sixteenth year of King Edwin*

By Candlemas there are four names on the steward's slate, and a fifth that nobody has written down.

Azenor de Kerguen, Tanguy's sister, twenty, with her mother's head for accounts and her father's temper. A lady of Mahaut's household at Lannec, with the Duchess's favour for a dowry. Cecily Hales, whose father keeps the King's accounts at Wendmere and whose dowry is in coin.

And the name nobody writes down, because it belongs to someone of no great house at all, whom you have known since you were children, and whom you look for in every room.

"Kerguen is the counterweight to Penhoët," says Piers atte Brook, who has opinions. "Mahaut is the law. Hales is money. The other one," he says, and stops, and looks at you, "is your own business."

- Marry Azenor de Kerguen.
- Marry into Mahaut's household, and the Armance.
- Marry the Hales money, and the King's ear with it.
- Marry the one whose name nobody wrote down.
- Put it off a year. There is time.

**You choose:** Marry Azenor de Kerguen.

Tanguy rides over himself to settle the terms, and drinks your wine, and laughs at the steward's jokes, and does not haggle once. "My mother's done the haggling already," he says. "By letter. You'll find it in your steward's chest." Hervé de Penhoët hears of it within the week. He sends congratulations.

*Penhoët: colder; Tanguy: much warmer; Kerguen: much warmer; Kerguen's standing: much higher; John marries Azenor de Kerguen*

### The Wedding Feast

*Spring 918, the seventeenth year of King Edwin*

You are married at Easter, in the chapel, with the household packed in to the door and the yard full of the people who could not get in. Then the feast: the long tables out, the boar's head, the musicians from Lannec, and the whole West, or as much of it as owes you or wants something, in one hall.

Hervé de Penhoët has come. He has come as kin, with Robert on one side of him and the Penhoët grandchild on the other, and he stands in the doorway waiting to be told where to sit. The steward looks at you. So do the old knights.

Further up the table Agnès is talking to Robert in the low, even voice that means trouble, and you can hear the word "dower" from where you sit.

- Seat Hervé at the high table, beside you.
- Seat him by his rank, and no higher.
- Have the steward tell him there is no room.

**You choose:** Seat him by his rank, and no higher.

The steward seats him by his rank, which is a fine and complicated thing, and puts him halfway down the hall between a bishop's nephew and the Kerguen steward. Hervé bows to you across the hall when the toasts come. He looks entirely content, which is the thing about Hervé.

### The Dower

*Spring 918, the seventeenth year of King Edwin*

Agnès comes to you after the wedding with a clerk from her own family and a roll of parchment.

"My dower," she says. "A third of the house's land, for my life, as the custom is. Your father's will gave it to me, and I'd like it measured and set out now, while we're all still speaking." She looks at your new lady across the hall. "Before there are more mouths at this table to argue about it."

The clerk unrolls the parchment. Her family has already chosen which third.

- Give her the third her family chose, and the house at the ford to live in.
- Give the third the custom allows, measured by the steward, not the clerk. [Favorable]

**You choose:** Give her the third her family chose, and the house at the ford to live in.

Agnès looks at you, and then at the clerk, who is visibly disappointed not to have to argue. "Well," she says. "Your father would have made me fight for it." She kisses you on the cheek, which she has not done since you were small.

*Coin −£10; The household: more loyal; Michaelmas, a fair harvest and steady trade: £255 15s came in; the household £25 11s 6d, the men's pay £9 and repairs £20 went out*

### Robert

*Autumn 918, the seventeenth year of King Edwin*

After Michaelmas Robert comes to see you from Penhoët, riding alone, with the boar on his saddlecloth. He is twenty-six now, and he has your father's way of standing in a doorway as if he owns it.

"Half," he says. "Father wanted me to have the ford, and the land south of the river, and you talked him down to half. Everyone says it was his idea." He looks at you. "Was it?"

Hervé, he does not say, would give him land in a heartbeat, and would want to know what he would give for it.

- Give him a manor of his own, and mean it.
- Give him coin now, and a promise for later. (£10)
- Tell him the house cannot spare it.

**You choose:** Give him a manor of his own, and mean it.

"The Saltings," you say: the manor at the marsh's edge, with two villages and a stretch of the salt, that the steward has kept on his maps since before either of you was born. Robert looks at you for a long moment, and then laughs, and then, to your great surprise, cries a little, and is furious about it. The steward says nothing for two days. That is what it cost.

*Robert: much closer; Coin −£10*

### Robert Comes Home

*Winter 918, the seventeenth year of King Edwin*

At Christmas Robert comes home for the feast, from Penhoët, with the Penhoët children in the cart and Hervé's good wishes and a cask of the thin sour wine.

It is a good Christmas. On the third night, when the hall has gone to bed, he sits with you by the fire and says what he came to say.

"Hervé wants me to carry Penhoët's claim," he says. "If the Estates write the male line, my children have a right in the Armance. He wants me to say so, when the time comes, in the hall at Lannec." He looks at the fire. "You'll want me to say the opposite. I can see it in your face."

- Ask him to work for the house, from inside Penhoët. [Even]
- Tell him to do what is right for his children.

**You choose:** Ask him to work for the house, from inside Penhoët.

"You're asking me to spy on my own children's grandfather," he says. "At Christmas." He goes to bed. In the morning he is civil, and the Penhoët cart leaves before dinner.

*Robert: further off*

### Old Knights, New Men

*Summer 919, the eighteenth year of King Edwin*

The muster at midsummer, in the long meadow, the way your father held it every year. The knights ride in with their men, and the steward counts them, and you sit your horse at the end of the field and watch.

They are old. You had not seen it, quite, until you saw them all together in the sun: grey beards under the helms, stiff knees in the stirrups, a man you have known all your life who has to be helped onto his horse. They rode with your father when the house was nothing. They are what the house was built from.

Behind them, at the edge of the field, there are younger men watching: younger sons of the West with good horses and no land, a free company's captain who has come to see whether you are hiring, the reeve's eldest boy with a borrowed sword. None of them has ever knelt to anyone in this house.

Piers atte Brook rides up beside you, and looks where you are looking, and says nothing for once.

- Keep the old knights in their places. They earned them.
- Raise new men, the Keeper's own.
- Pension the oldest off with honour, and fill their places slowly. (£12)

**You choose:** Pension the oldest off with honour, and fill their places slowly. (£12)

A pension for life to each of the five oldest, and a place at the high table at Christmas, and their sons first in line for their fathers' service. It costs a great deal. The old men grumble, and take it, and drink to you at Christmas, and mean it.

*Hal's shadow: shorter; Coin −£12*

### The Keeper's Accounts

*Summer 919, the eighteenth year of King Edwin*

At midsummer you go through the accounts with the steward, all of them, for the first time as the one who has to answer for them. Last Michaelmas brought a fair harvest and steady trade. Your father left the house a great house, and the rolls say what that is worth: so much from Kerval, so much from the holdings, so much from the knights, and the household and the men's pay out of it before you see a penny.

"There's money for one thing this year, my lord," says the steward. "Perhaps two. Not all of them."

You have £1500 14s 6d.

- Build a stone bridge over the stream below Kerval, and toll it. (£75)
- Rebuild the church at Kerval in stone, with a tower. (£40)
- Put in a cider press, and new trees where the old ones failed. (£24)
- Lend Kerguen fifty pounds at ten in the hundred. (£50)
- Arm six men of the village as a watch, and pay them. (£6)
- Keep the money in the chest.

**You choose:** Rebuild the church at Kerval in stone, with a tower. (£40)

It takes two summers and a master mason from Lannec who drinks. The tower can be seen from Penhoët land, which was, you tell the priest, not the point.

*Coin −£40; Church and priest +2; Kerval: the village warmer; Standing with the Church: higher; Standing with Piety: higher*

### Sir Josselin's Daughter

*Autumn 919, the eighteenth year of King Edwin*

Sir Josselin of the dyke comes at Michaelmas with his hat in his hands and his daughter behind him, a tall girl of fifteen with ink on her fingers. He owes the Lannec moneylenders forty pounds, and cannot pay it, and has come to the greatest house he knows.

"Take her into your household," he says. "She can read and reckon better than my steward. Better than me. In a great house she'll meet a husband I could never find her." He does not look at his daughter while he says it. She looks at you.

- Take her into the household, and forgive the debt.
- Lend him what he needs, at no interest, and send them both home. (£10)
- Tell him the house cannot.

**You choose:** Take her into the household, and forgive the debt.

Her name is Perrine. Within a year she is keeping the household's accounts, and finding things in them the steward would rather she had not, and the steward has started, grudgingly, to ask her to check his sums.

*The household: more loyal*

### Before the Estates

*Spring 920, the nineteenth year of King Edwin*

At Lady Day the word comes from Lannec: the King's justices will sit at Whitsun to hear the matter of the West's liberties, and of the Duchess of Armance's daughter, who is fourteen, and whose wardship King Edwin claims by the law of Adalia.

The governor's man comes to the house before the week is out. The King would be glad, he says, if the house would speak for the King's right. The King has a long memory for those who are glad to help him.

Mahaut's steward comes three days later, with a letter in her own hand. Her daughter, she writes, is not a horse to be sold at Wendmere. She asks the house to remember Lannec, and the West's liberties, and who stood first. She offers, if the house speaks for her right, a place for Robert in her council.

You put the two offers side by side on the table in the hall, and look at them, and do not answer either. Not yet.

Nobody tells you any more what your father would have done. They are waiting to see what you will.

- Go on.

**You choose:** Go on.

### The End

Here Act I of Book One ends. You are head of the house Hal Fletcher founded, under King Edwin, married to Azenor de Kerguen, with Kerval in your hands, and two offers on the table for the house's vote.
